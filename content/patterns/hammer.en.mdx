Price has been falling for days. Then one candle drops even lower, turns around and closes near its high. Many traders buy on sight and get stopped out two candles later. The hammer is a real signal, but only in the right place. This guide shows you that place and the rules around it.

## At a glance

| Item             | Hammer                                                                                   |
| :--------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Type             | Bullish reversal, one candle                                                             |
| Where to look    | End of a downtrend or pullback, at support or a demand zone                              |
| Key features     | Long lower wick (at least 2× the body), small body near the top, little or no upper wick |
| Confirmation     | Next candle trades or closes above the hammer high                                       |
| Stop             | Below the hammer low, plus a spread buffer                                               |
| Invalidation     | A close below the hammer low                                                             |
| Look-alikes      | Hanging Man (same shape at the top of an uptrend), Dragonfly Doji                        |
| Opposite pattern | Shooting Star                                                                            |

## What you will be able to do

- **Identify** a hammer with three measurements.
- **Tell** a strong hammer from a weak one.
- **Explain** the difference between a hammer and a hanging man.
- **Place** entry, stop and target step by step.
- **Spot** the hammers you should skip before you take them.

## Why this matters

- **It is everywhere.** Hammers appear on almost every chart and timeframe, so a reading mistake repeats often.
- **The stop is obvious.** The hammer low is a clear line: below it, the idea is wrong. You can size risk in advance.
- **It is not enough alone.** A hammer in open space is often just a pause in the trend. Without location, this pattern costs money.

## What is a hammer

**A hammer is a candle with a long lower wick and a small body near the top, formed at support after a decline.**

Throw a ball hard at the floor: the harder it falls, the harder it bounces. The long lower wick is the fall. The high close is the bounce off the floor. With no floor, meaning no support, the ball keeps falling.

### Recognition rules

| Rule                                    | Required?                   | Why                                             |
| :-------------------------------------- | :-------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- |
| Prior downtrend or pullback             | Yes                         | No decline, no reversal                         |
| Lower wick at least 2× the body         | Yes                         | Measures how strongly the lows were rejected    |
| Body in the upper third of the range    | Yes                         | A high close means buyers had the last word     |
| No or tiny upper wick                   | Yes                         | A big upper wick means sellers also hit the top |
| Green body                              | No                          | Slightly stronger if green; red still counts    |
| At support, demand or a prior swing low | No (but close to essential) | Location sets the weight of the signal          |

The "2×" rule is the common definition in candlestick literature (Steve Nison). It is a definition, not a statistic.

| Quality check | Strong                                                   | Weak                        |
| :------------ | :------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------- |
| Lower wick    | 3× the body or more                                      | Exactly 2× or less          |
| Close         | Within the top 25% of the range                          | Middle of the range         |
| Size          | Wider range than the last 5-10 candles                   | Same size as its neighbours |
| Low           | Dips slightly below support or a prior low, then returns | Nowhere near a level        |

<Callout type="note">
  "3×" and "top 25%" are my working thresholds, not verified statistics. Test
  them in your own backtest, set your own limits, then apply the same limits on
  every chart.
</Callout>

| Pattern             | Shape                                   | Where             | Meaning                                                |
| :------------------ | :-------------------------------------- | :---------------- | :----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hammer**          | Long lower wick, body on top            | After a decline   | Sellers failed at the lows: bullish                    |
| **Hanging Man**     | Identical                               | Top of an uptrend | Sellers could push price down even at the top: warning |
| **Dragonfly Doji**  | Open = close = high, long lower wick    | Anywhere          | No body: even less decisive                            |
| **Inverted Hammer** | Long **upper** wick, body at the bottom | After a decline   | Buyers tried higher: needs more confirmation           |

**The shape is the same; what came before decides the name.** So read the 10-20 candles before it first.

## The story inside the candle

Broken down over time, a hammer is commonly read like this:

1. **Sellers push after the open.** The trend is down, so early selling is normal. Price drops a long way.
2. **New buyers appear lower.** Price reaches support or trades below a prior low. Waiting buyers step in. Many sellers keep their [stop loss](/en/learn/10-trade-management-execution/01-stop-loss-placement) under the prior low; when those trigger, they become buy orders too.
3. **Price recovers before the close.** Traders who sold the lows are now losing. Some buy back to exit, lifting price further.

Result: by the close, price is back near where it started. Sellers had the whole session and could not hold the lows. That is the message of a hammer.

<Callout type="note">
  This is a common interpretation, not a proven fact. A chart does not show who
  bought. We only see how price behaved.
</Callout>

## Step by step: trading a hammer

<Steps>

### Step 1: Location first

Mark support, demand zones and prior swing lows before the hammer appears. If you look for a level after the fact, any spot will look like one.

### Step 2: Wait for the close

A live candle can look like a hammer and still close as a plain bearish candle. Only count closed candles.

### Step 3: Check the three measurements

Lower wick at least 2× the body? Close in the upper third? Small upper wick? One "no" means it is not a hammer.

### Step 4: Choose an entry type

| Entry                     | Where                                         | Pro                          | Con                           |
| :------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------- | :---------------------------- |
| **High break** (standard) | Buy stop just above the hammer high           | Price proves direction first | Later entry, bigger stop      |
| **Close entry**           | Market buy as the hammer closes               | Best price                   | No confirmation               |
| **50% retest**            | Buy limit at the midpoint of the hammer range | Small stop, better R:R       | Missed if price never returns |

The high break is the simplest for beginners: if the next candle does not rise, there is no trade.

### Step 5: Place the stop

Below the hammer low. Add at least the [spread](/en/learn/01-foundations/04-spread-commission-swap) and a few pips, because the low is now the most watched point on the chart.

### Step 6: Size and target

Measure entry-to-stop distance and calculate [position size](/en/learn/08-risk-management/02-position-sizing) so the loss stays at or below 1% of the account. Put the target just before the nearest resistance or prior swing high. If the [risk reward](/en/learn/08-risk-management/03-risk-reward) is below 1:1.5, skip the trade.

### Step 7: Manage

At 1R in profit you may consider moving the stop to break-even. If a candle closes below the hammer low, the idea is wrong: exit without waiting.

</Steps>

### Pre-trade checklist

<Checklist
  id="pattern-hammer-pretrade-en"

    "Has the candle closed?",
    "Is the risk reward to the nearest resistance at least 1:1.5?",
    "Is there no higher-timeframe resistance right above the entry?",
  ]}
/>

## Example 1: when it works (illustrative)

<Chart
  id="hammer-win-01"
  height={420}
  caption="GBPUSD H4 (illustrative, synthetic) — a hammer at support that held once before; the next candle breaks the hammer high."
/>

- **What was visible:** Candle 5 set support at 1.2700. Candles 11 to 18 made lower highs back down to it.
- **The hammer:** Candle 19 opened at 1.2716, dropped to 1.2668 and closed at 1.2732. Range 69 pips, body 16, lower wick 48: three times the body. Upper wick only 5. The low went 32 pips below support and came back.
- **Decision:** Buy stop at 1.2739 (2 pips above the high). Stop at 1.2660 (8 pips below the low): 79 pips of risk. Target 1.2897, which is 2R.
- **Result:** Candle 20 broke the high and filled the entry. Price never came near the stop. Candle 30 reached the target.
- **Lesson:** The level existed beforehand, the shape met the rules, and the next candle proved direction. All three lined up.

## Example 2: when it is a trap (illustrative)

<Chart
  id="hammer-fail-01"
  height={420}
  caption="GBPUSD H4 (illustrative, synthetic) — a hammer in open space during a momentum drop. The entry fills, then the stop is hit."
/>

- **What was visible:** Candles 5 to 9 were five large bearish candles. No marked support below.
- **The hammer:** Candle 10 had a perfect shape: range 52 pips, lower wick 38, body 10, upper wick 4.
- **The wrong decision:** "The shape fits." Buy stop at 1.2708, stop at 1.2646: 62 pips of risk.
- **What happened:** Candle 11 wicked to 1.2713 and filled the entry, but closed at 1.2696, below the hammer close. Candle 13 broke the stop. Price found real support near 1.2580.
- **Why it failed:** There was no level below, and five large bearish candles showed strong sellers. One rejection candle could not reverse that momentum.

**Lesson:** A perfect shape in the wrong place is still the wrong trade. Because the stop was set in advance, the loss stayed at 1R (62 pips).

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                 | Why it happens                       | What to do instead                                              |
| :-------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Calling any long lower wick a hammer    | The eye sees shape, not measurements | Check all three: 2× wick, high close, small upper wick          |
| Ignoring the prior trend                | Looking at the candle alone          | Read the prior 10-20 candles; in an uptrend it is a hanging man |
| Entering on a live candle               | Fear of missing out                  | Wait for the close                                              |
| Stop exactly at the hammer low          | Wanting a small stop                 | Add spread plus a buffer below the low                          |
| Buying in the middle of a momentum drop | "It has fallen enough"               | Level first, hammer second                                      |
| Trading without a target                | Too much faith in the pattern        | Measure R:R to the nearest resistance first                     |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  When the hammer low dips slightly below a prior swing low and returns, price
  has taken out the stops resting there and come back. This is a [liquidity
  sweep](/en/learn/03-market-structure/04-liquidity-stop-hunts), and it adds
  weight to the hammer. Example 1 is exactly this.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
  See a hammer on H1? Open H4 or Daily. If price sits just under resistance
  there, the small hammer's target will hit that wall. See [Multi-timeframe
  analysis](/en/learn/03-market-structure/05-multi-timeframe-analysis).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
  Do not accept claims like "hammers win 70% of the time" without checking.
  Results depend on market, timeframe, location and how you count. Your own
  backtest is the only number you can trust.
</Callout>

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- Hammer = long lower wick (at least 2× the body), small body on top, almost no upper wick, after a decline.
- The same shape at the top of an uptrend is a hanging man. The prior trend sets the name.
- Level first, shape second, trigger third (break of the hammer high).
- Stop below the hammer low with a buffer. Size from the stop distance.
- A perfect hammer in open space can still be a trap.

</KeyTakeaways>

| :--- | :------------- | :-------------- | :---------------------- | :----------- | :--------- |
|      |                |                 |                         |              |            |

Then split them into "level present" and "no level". Compare the average R of the two groups. Collect at least 30 samples before drawing conclusions. Method: [How to Backtest](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/01-how-to-backtest).

2. After a hammer, the next candle closes below the hammer low. What do you do?
3. What is the main difference between a high-break entry and a 50% retest entry?
4. Why did the hammer in Example 2 fail despite its perfect shape?

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1.** A hanging man. Same shape as a hammer, but at the top of an uptrend. The prior trend sets the name.

**2.** The pattern is cancelled. Exit if you are in; do not enter if you are not.

**3.** The high break waits for price to prove direction but needs a bigger stop. The 50% retest has a smaller stop and better R:R, but you miss the trade if price never returns.

**4.** There was no level below, and one candle could not reverse the momentum of five large bearish candles.
