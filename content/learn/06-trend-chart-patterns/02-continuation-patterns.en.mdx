# Continuation Patterns: Flag, Triangle, Wedge and Pennant

A trend is running and you want to get in, but price has moved so fast that there is no place left to catch it. Then price stops and starts moving around in a narrow box. Most people think "the trend is over." Often it is not. **This is a continuation pattern: the shape of a trend catching its breath in the middle.**

## What you will be able to do

- **Tell apart** a flag, a pennant, a triangle and a wedge.
- **Understand** whether a pause is normal or **a sign of reversal**.
- **Set** the entry, stop and target on a breakout **from the size of the pattern**.
- **Recognise** when a pattern **has failed** and keep the loss small.

## Why it matters

- **You get a second chance to enter with the trend.** Even if you missed the first push, you can get in after the pause, and the stop stays close.
- **You can tell a pause from a reversal.** If you do not know this difference, you will get scared and exit on every pullback.
- **The target can be measured.** The target comes from the length of the earlier move.

Prerequisites: [Reversal Patterns](/en/learn/06-trend-chart-patterns/01-reversal-patterns) and [Trendlines and Channels](/en/learn/05-support-resistance/04-trendlines-channels).

## What a continuation pattern is

**A continuation pattern is a narrowing, quiet part in the middle of a running trend. When it breaks, the trend usually keeps going in the same direction.**

Think of someone climbing stairs, standing at the turn in the middle and catching their breath. They have stopped, but they are not going down. **Stopping and going back are not the same thing; this is the core of the pattern.**

There is one condition: there must be **a strong move before the pattern**. Without the move, the narrow shape is just a range.

## The four patterns at a glance

| Pattern  | Shape                                                    | Usual length  | Break direction (in most cases) |
| :------- | :------------------------------------------------------- | :------------ | :------------------------------ |
| Flag     | After the pole, a parallel box sloping against the trend | 5-15 candles  | With the trend                  |
| Pennant  | After the pole, a small symmetrical triangle             | 5-15 candles  | With the trend                  |
| Triangle | Two lines leaning toward each other                      | 15-40 candles | With the trend (usually)        |
| Wedge    | Two lines sloping the same way, but toward each other    | 15-40 candles | Against the slope               |

- The **pole** is the sharp move before the pattern. The size and target of flags and pennants come from this pole.
- A **flag** and a **pennant** are almost the same. In a flag the two lines are parallel; in a pennant the lines lean toward each other.
- **Triangles** come in three types: ascending (flat top, rising lows), descending (flat bottom, falling highs) and symmetrical (pressure from both sides).
- The **wedge** is the most confusing. A rising wedge in an uptrend usually breaks down, which means it leans toward reversal. A falling wedge is the opposite. Think of a wedge as "strength running out", not a "pause".

Below, see the flag and the ascending triangle on charts; the same reasoning applies to wedges and pennants.

## What it looks like on a chart: Bull Flag

/>

It forms in four steps:

1. **Pole (0 to 8):** a sharp rise of 160 pips. Almost every candle goes the same way.
2. **Flag (9 to 17):** price falls slowly, with both highs and lows lower. But the fall is slow and the candles are small.
3. **Break (18):** the candle **closed** above the upper line of the flag.
4. **Trigger (19):** price broke the high of the break candle and moved on. Buy here.

**Do not panic at the downward slope of the flag.** Sellers are not coming in; buyers are only taking profit. There is a difference in quality: the pole's candles are big, the flag's candles are small.

## What it looks like on a chart: Ascending Triangle

<Chart
  id="triangle-ascending-01"
  height={440}
  caption="EURUSD H4 (educational, synthetic example): resistance at 1.0900 three times, higher lows below; candle 22 closes above, retest on candle 24, buy on candle 25"
/>

- **Flat top:** sellers stand at the same price, 1.0900 (touched on candles 5, 11 and 17).
- **Rising bottom:** buyers buy at a higher price each time (lows 1.0760, 1.0810, 1.0860).
- **Pressure builds:** the space gets narrower. Either the sellers give up, or the buyers let go.
- **Break (22):** the candle closed above 1.0900. Broken resistance is now support: this is [Role Reversal](/en/learn/05-support-resistance/02-role-reversal).

A descending triangle is the exact opposite: flat bottom, falling highs, and it usually breaks down. It is hard to say in advance which way a symmetrical triangle will break, so **wait for a break in the direction of the earlier trend**; do not guess.

## Step by step: trading a continuation breakout

<Steps>

### Step 1: Confirm the move and the trend first

Flags and pennants need a sharp pole before them. Triangles need at least one clear trend before them. If there is none, do not look for the pattern.

### Step 2: Measure the depth and length of the pause

A flag or pennant should take back **less than half** of the pole, and last only a few candles, not dozens. On the flag chart above, the pole is 160 pips, the pause 71 pips (about 44%) and 9 candles. If it is deeper or longer, it is no longer a pause.

### Step 3: Draw the two lines

Draw the upper line through at least two touches and the lower line through at least two touches. If you draw through the wicks, the line becomes a slightly thick zone, and that is fine.

### Step 4: Wait for the close

If a wick touches through, it is not a break. Check whether the candle **closed** outside the line and whether the body is strong.

### Step 5: Choose the entry

There are two ways:

- **Straight after the break:** when the high of the break candle breaks (on the flag chart, buy 1.0942).
- **On the retest:** if price comes back to the broken line and holds, when the retest candle's high breaks (on the triangle chart, buy 1.0924).

A retest usually makes the stop smaller, but it does not always come. If it does not, you can let it go.

### Step 6: Put the stop where you are proven wrong

- **Flag/pennant:** below the lowest low of the flag (1.0884).
- **Triangle:** if price closes back inside the broken level, the pattern is cancelled, so a little below the level (1.0884).

### Step 7: Measure the target

- **Flag/pennant:** the height of the pole, measured from the breakout point. In the example, 160 pips, so about 1.0909 + 160 = 1.1069, rounded to 1.1060.
- **Triangle:** the height of the widest part of the triangle, measured from the broken line. In the example, 140 pips, so 1.0900 + 140 = 1.1040.

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

The bull flag chart above:

- **What you could see:** a 160-pip pole, then a 9-candle downward flag, a 44% retrace.
- **Break:** candle 18 closed at 1.0936, past the upper line of the flag (about 1.0909).
- **Trigger:** candle 19 broke the high of candle 18 (1.0940). Buy at 1.0942.
- **Stop:** below the flag's low at 1.0884, a risk of 58 pips.
- **Target:** 1.1060, a distance of 118 pips, which is about 2.0 times the risk. More in the [Risk/Reward Ratio](/en/learn/08-risk-management/03-risk-reward) lesson.
- **Result:** Price rose step by step and reached the target on candle 27.
- **Lesson:** If you had bought inside the flag, you would have had to keep the stop much lower, and you would not have known whether price would break out.

The same reasoning on the triangle chart, with a different entry: getting in on the retest, the stop is 40 pips and the target 116 pips, which is about 2.9 times.

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

The same pole, but the "flag" is no longer a flag.

<Chart
  id="flag-fail-01"
  height={440}
  caption="EURUSD H4 (educational, synthetic example): falling for ten candles and taking back 61% of the pole; candle 18 peeks above with a small body, candle 19 hits the stop"
/>

- **What you could see:** the same 160-pip pole. The "flag" fell 98 pips, about 61%, over ten candles.
- **Decision:** Candle 18 crossed the upper line with a small body. Buy on the break of its high at 1.0899, stop 1.0854, a risk of 45 pips.
- **What happened:** Candle 19 went up, came back and closed at 1.0858. The stop was filled. After that, price kept falling.
- **Why it failed:** Four signs were present together: the pause was too deep, too long, the break had a small body, and there was no momentum. The buyers' strength had run out.
- **Lesson:** A pole does not make a flag on its own. A deep and long pause says sellers are coming back. The loss was the planned 45 pips. ## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                          | Why it happens                             | What to do                                                     |
| :----------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Looking for a flag with no pole                  | Thinking every sideways move is a pattern  | Measure the move first                                         |
| Getting in even when the pause is deep or long   | "The pattern is there"                     | Skip it if it retraces more than half or the pause is long     |
| Getting in early, inside the pattern             | Wanting a better price by getting in first | Wait until a close goes outside                                |
| Treating a wedge as a pause                      | The lines slope, so it looks like a trend  | A rising wedge in an uptrend leans toward reversal; be careful |
| Assuming the direction of a symmetrical triangle | Bias toward one side                       | Look at the close, then decide                                 |
| Getting in without measuring the target          | "Let it go as far as it goes"              | Measure and check R:R; skip if too low                         |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  It is good if volume or the size of the candles slowly shrinks while the
  pattern forms. Getting quieter means pressure is building. If the candles stay
  big and messy, the pattern is unclear.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
  This reasoning is more reliable on higher timeframes. A flag on H4 has less
  noise than a flag on H1. Match it with the direction of the big trend in
  [Multi-Timeframe
  Analysis](/en/learn/03-market-structure/05-multi-timeframe-analysis).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
  A continuation pattern breaks with the trend "usually", not "always". There is
  always a chance of a break the other way. Do not enter this pattern without a
  stop.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **When there was no strong move before:** the pole is the base of flags and pennants.
- **When the pause is too much:** If it is too long or too deep, it is not a pause in the trend but a change.
- **Around news:** A sudden spike breaks through the line and comes back, so you cannot read the break.
- **Near the end of a trend:** In a trend that has come a long way, continuation and reversal look almost the same. Check whether there is [Resistance](/en/learn/05-support-resistance/01-levels-vs-zones) nearby.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- A continuation pattern is a quiet, narrowing pause after a strong move.
- In flags and pennants the target comes from the pole; in triangles, from the height of the pattern.
- Entry after a close outside the line, stop where the pattern is cancelled.
- A deep, long or momentum-less pause is not a flag but a warning of reversal.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Quiz

1. What is the difference between a flag and a pennant?
2. How do you measure the target of a flag?
3. Why is it better not to call a ten-candle pause that takes back 60% of the pole a flag?
4. What does a rising wedge in the middle of an uptrend usually mean?

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1.** In a flag the two lines are parallel; in a pennant the lines lean toward each other (a small triangle).

**2.** Measure the height of the pole, then place the target that distance from the breakout point in the direction of the trend.

**3.** A pause that is too deep and too long says the sellers have come back. In a trend that has lost strength, breaks often fail.

**4.** Strength is running out. It usually breaks down, so it is a signal that leans toward reversal.
