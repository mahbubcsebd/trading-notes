# Entry, Exit and Risk Rules: Rules With Numbers on One Page

"Enter at a good place, exit at the right place." It sounds nice, but which place is good? In this lesson you will learn to turn a vague wish into numbers and conditions.

## What you will be able to do

- **Write entry, exit and risk** rules separately.
- **Break a rule into yes/no conditions.**
- **Check** whether a trade **followed the rules**.

## Why it matters

- **Vague rules are easy to break.** It is easy to enter by saying "it almost matched".
- **Results can be compared.** Trades taken by the same rules are the ones fit for measuring [expectancy](/en/learn/08-risk-management/07-expectancy).
- **The risk is fixed in advance.** If you set the lot after placing the stop, the loss stays as planned.

Prerequisites: [Trading Plan Template](/en/learn/14-trading-plan-system/01-trading-plan-template) and [Stop Loss Placement](/en/learn/10-trade-management-execution/01-stop-loss-placement).

## Three groups of rules

**Entry rules say when you enter, exit rules say when you leave, and risk rules say how much you are willing to lose.** Written together, the three define a trade completely.

| Group | Question                     | Kind of rule (imaginary example)                                                  |
| :---- | :--------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Entry | Have all the conditions met? | H1 close above resistance; price has retested; a bullish rejection candle         |
| Exit  | Where wrong, where profit?   | Stop 5 pips below the retest low; target 2R; no moving the stop before the target |
| Risk  | How much risk?               | 1% of the account; lot = risk in money ÷ stop pip value                           |

The test for writing a rule: **can someone else look at your chart and say "yes" or "no"?** If so, the rule is good.

## Step by step: writing the rules

<Steps>

### Step 1: Count the entry conditions

Three to four yes/no conditions. If not all are "yes", do not enter. This is the job of the [Entry Model Checklist](/en/learn/09-price-action-entries-strategies/05-entry-model-checklist).

### Step 2: The stop rule

Based on what: swing low, retest low, ATR. Write "below what" and not "how many pips".

### Step 3: The target rule

A fixed R (such as 2R) or the next level. Following [Risk Reward](/en/learn/08-risk-management/03-risk-reward), write the minimum ratio.

### Step 4: The lot formula

Risk in money ÷ (stop pips × pip value). From [Position Sizing](/en/learn/08-risk-management/02-position-sizing).

### Step 5: Forbidden acts

Moving the stop farther away, raising size, entering outside the plan. Write them in three lines.

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **Rules:** H1 close above 1.0960, buy on the retest, stop 30 pips, target 2R.
- **While running:** after the breakout price came down and touched 1.0960. A rejection candle formed. Buy 1.0968, stop 1.0938, target 1.1028.
- **Result:** the target was hit, +2R.
- **Lesson:** all three prices were set in advance. The win was due not to luck but to a repeatable process.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

/>

- **What was visible:** the same setup. But the wait for the retest was not tolerated, and the buy was on the breakout candle itself.
- **Result:** buy 1.1000, the stop at 1.0970 was hit, -1R.
- **Why it failed:** with one condition (the retest) dropped, the entry moved away from the level. A normal pullback alone touched the stop.
- **Lesson:** even if the setup is right, dropping one rule changes the result. This loss is not "the market's fault". It is "the result of breaking a rule".

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                             | Why it happens               | What to do                       |
| :---------------------------------- | :--------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| Entering on "almost matched"        | Fear of missing a chance     | If not all are yes, skip         |
| Not writing the reason for the stop | Placing it by counting pips  | Write "below what"               |
| Setting the target by the mood      | Greed                        | Write a fixed R or a level first |
| Putting in the lot by guess         | The math feels like a bother | Write the formula down           |
| Changing the rules after a loss     | Emotion                      | No change before 20 trades       |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  Take a screenshot before the trade, with entry, stop and target. Later match
  whether the rules were followed. This is a key ingredient of the
  [journal](/en/learn/12-trading-psychology/05-journaling-habit).
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
  Before adding a new rule, ask: if it had been there for the last 20 trades,
  would the result have changed? If you do not know the answer, it is a guess.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **If you write the rules carefully but do not test the setup:** a wrong rule that is written down is still wrong.
- **At the moment of news:** if price jumps, entry and stop may not be at the written prices ([slippage](/en/learn/10-trade-management-execution/04-slippage-requote-spread-widening)).
- **If there are too many rules:** more than ten conditions almost never match together, so no trade happens at all.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- Write rules in three groups: entry, exit and risk.
- Each rule should be measurable by yes/no or a number.
- Dropping even one rule changes the result.
- Entry, stop and target are known before the trade.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Practice

For your favorite setup, write the rules in the three groups. Apply them to the last three charts: can you say yes/no to each condition?

## Quiz

1. How do you test whether a rule is good?
2. If two of the three entry conditions match, what do you do?
3. Why write "below what" in the stop rule?
4. What was the cause of the loss above?

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1.** If someone else can look at the chart and say yes/no, the rule is good.

**2.** Do not enter. All conditions must be "yes".

**3.** Because the market changes. If there is a reason (swing low, retest low), the stop stays meaningful.

**4.** Entering on the breakout candle without waiting for the retest, which is breaking the rule.

</details>
