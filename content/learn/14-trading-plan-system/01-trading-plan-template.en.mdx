# Trading Plan Template: Written Rules on One Page

If you drive in a strange city without a map, you have to stop at every turn and think. It is the same in trading: with no plan, a new decision at every candle, and the more decisions, the more mistakes. In this lesson you will learn to build a one-page plan.

## What you will be able to do

- **Recognize the six parts** of a trading plan.
- **Fill in a one-page template** for yourself.
- **Tell good writing from bad writing** in a plan.

## Why it matters

- **In a moment of emotion there is no time to think.** [Discipline](/en/learn/12-trading-psychology/01-discipline) is possible only when what to do is already written.
- **You can check the results.** Without rules you cannot tell whether the result came from the strategy or from luck.
- **The later lessons stand on it.** The checklist, routine and stop rules are all parts of this document.

Prerequisites: [Risk per Trade](/en/learn/08-risk-management/01-risk-per-trade) and [Entry Model Checklist](/en/learn/09-price-action-entries-strategies/05-entry-model-checklist).

## What is a trading plan

**A trading plan is a list of rules written in advance that says what you trade, when you enter and exit, how much risk you take and when you stop.**

Think of a plane's checklist. However experienced the pilot is, they run through the list every time, because relying on the mind is the riskiest thing. Your plan is your checklist.

## The six parts

| #   | Part                   | Main question                                 | Example (imaginary)                           |
| :-- | :--------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Market and timeframe   | What do I trade, on which chart?              | EURUSD, H1 (setup), M15 (entry)               |
| 2   | Setup                  | In what situation do I enter?                 | Retest after a resistance break               |
| 3   | Entry and trigger      | On exactly which signal do I buy/sell?        | A bullish rejection candle on the retest      |
| 4   | Exit: stop and target  | Where am I proved wrong, where is the profit? | Stop 30 pips below, target 2R                 |
| 5   | Risk                   | How much am I willing to lose?                | 1% per trade, at most 2% a day                |
| 6   | Stop rules and routine | When do I close?                              | Stop after 2 losses in a day, a 2-hour window |

Write each part with **a number or a yes/no question**. "I'll enter if the market looks good" is not a rule. It is an opinion.

The plan's job is at the moment of this pullback. The mind says "let me get out"; the plan says "the stop has not been hit yet".

## Step by step: writing a one-page plan

<Steps>

### Step 1: Start with one market and one timeframe

Pick one instrument at first. Look at the [Trading Sessions](/en/learn/01-foundations/05-trading-sessions) and write the active time.

### Step 2: Pick one setup

One that you can write in clear language, like [Breakout and Retest](/en/learn/09-price-action-entries-strategies/01-breakout-retest-pullback-entry). No more than three.

### Step 3: Rules for entry, stop and target

Set "how", not "at what price". For example: "stop 5 pips below the retest low".

### Step 4: Put in the risk numbers

The limit per trade and per day. Take them from [Daily and Weekly Limits](/en/learn/08-risk-management/05-daily-weekly-limits).

### Step 5: Write the stop rules

After how many losses the day ends, and after how many the week. More in lesson 5.

### Step 6: Shorten it to one page

Print the page and keep it on the desk. If it does not fit on one page, the plan is too complex.

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **Plan:** EURUSD H1, buy on the retest after a resistance break, stop 30 pips, target 2R, risk 1%.
- **While running:** all the conditions matched on the retest. Buy 1.0930, stop 1.0885, target 1.1020. A pullback came, and it did not touch the stop.
- **Result:** the target was hit, +2R.
- **Lesson:** a plan does not guarantee a win; it keeps the mind steady.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

- **What was seen:** the plan was written, but vague: "buy if it feels strong".
- **Result:** in the same week the trader took three different kinds of trade. Two losses, one small win. It could not be said which was the plan's.
- **Why it failed:** "if it feels strong" cannot be measured. So a broken plan cannot be caught either.
- **Lesson:** write each rule so that someone else, looking at the chart, can say whether you followed it.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                     | Why it happens              | What to do                                                        |
| :------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Vague rules                                 | They feel easy              | Write them as numbers or yes/no questions                         |
| Many setups at once                         | Fear of missing chances     | One at first, at most three                                       |
| Writing the plan and leaving it in a drawer | Writing feels like the work | Run your eye over it before every trade                           |
| Leaving out the risk part                   | Entry is the fun part       | A plan with no risk line is incomplete                            |
| Changing the plan during a trade            | Emotion                     | If you want to change it, do it after the trade, as a new version |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  Write the date and version in the plan (v1, v2). Write the reason for a change
  in one line. A few months later you will see which change helped.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
  A plan is not finished once written. Review it once a month by looking at the
  [Journal](/en/learn/12-trading-psychology/05-journaling-habit), but never
  during a trade.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **If the setup has not been tested:** written rules do not make a bad strategy good. First [backtest](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/01-how-to-backtest).
- **If there are so many rules you can't remember them:** that is the reason for the one-page limit.
- **In an unusual market:** in big news or a gap, even a written stop does not fully protect. Keep the risk low.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- The plan has six parts: market, setup, entry, exit, risk, stop rules.
- Write each rule so that it can be measured.
- The plan is written before the trade, not during it.
- If it does not fit on one page, simplify.

</KeyTakeaways>

1. What are the six parts of a trading plan?
2. Why is "I'll enter if the market looks good" not a rule?
3. When is it right to change the plan?
4. Does a plan guarantee a win?

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1.** Market and timeframe, setup, entry, exit, risk, stop rules and routine.

**2.** Because it cannot be measured. It can mean something different each time.
