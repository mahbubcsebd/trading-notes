# Liquidity and Stop Hunts: Why Price Touches Your Stop and Turns Back

Have you ever seen price race the other way right after your stop was hit? That is not bad luck. Often there is a specific reason behind it: **liquidity** had built up where your stop was.

## What you will be able to do

- **Explain** in your own words what **liquidity** and a **liquidity pool** are.
- **Recognise** **equal highs/lows** and the pools next to swings on a chart.
- **Tell apart** a **liquidity sweep** and a real **BOS**.
- **Place** your own **stop** where the crowd is thinner.

## Why it matters

- Most new traders keep their stops in the same obvious places. That is where price sweeps again and again.
- If you recognise a sweep, you stop mistaking a wick for a break, which is the main warning of the [BOS and CHOCH](/en/learn/03-market-structure/03-break-of-structure) lesson.
- If you set your stop location well, you get stopped out for no reason less often.

## What is liquidity?

Put simply, liquidity means **orders waiting at that price**. If someone wants to buy, someone else has to sell against them, or no trade happens. Where many orders have built up, large trades get filled easily.

**Analogy:** at a fair's gate, where the crowd is biggest, a large group wanting to get in has an advantage, because it can deal with many people at once. In the market, that crowd is the orders that have built up.

**Precise definition:** A **liquidity pool** is a price area just above or below which many stop losses and pending orders have built up. A stop loss is filled as a market order in the opposite direction. So a buyer's stop below is a sell order, and a seller's stop above is a buy order.

<Callout type="note">
  The idea that "big institutions hunt your stop on purpose" is very common
  among SMC teachers, but we offer no proof of it here. In this lesson we only
  learn the behaviour you can observe: price often touches the areas where stops
  have built up and turns back. Whatever the reason, recognising it helps you.
</Callout>

## Where liquidity builds up

<Steps>

### 1. Equal highs / equal lows

When two or more swings form at about the same price, everyone sees it as "clean" support or resistance. Buyers' stops build up below equal lows, and sellers' stops build up above equal highs. The swings from the earlier [swing high and swing low](/en/learn/03-market-structure/01-swing-high-low) lesson are the target here.

### 2. Beyond previous swings

Stops sit above important swing highs and below swing lows, because those are the most common places to put a stop.

### 3. Beyond a trendline

Stops build up below or above a clear trendline. See [trendlines and channels](/en/learn/05-support-resistance/04-trendlines-channels).

</Steps>

## Sweep vs real break

|                 | Liquidity sweep                | Real break (BOS)                    |
| :-------------- | :----------------------------- | :---------------------------------- |
| **Wick**        | Goes past the level            | Goes past the level                 |
| **Body close**  | Comes back inside              | Closes outside                      |
| **Next candle** | Quickly goes the other way     | Keeps going the same way or retests |
| **Meaning**     | The built-up stops were filled | New structure is forming            |

<Callout type="warning">
  A sweep is easy to recognise after the fact. While the candle is running, you
  do not know whether it is a sweep or a real break. So wait for the body close,
  and do not jump the other way thinking "every wick is a sweep".
</Callout>

## Step by step: using a sweep as a trade clue

<Steps>

### Step 1: Look at the big picture

Set the trend and direction on the higher timeframe. See [Multi-Timeframe Analysis](/en/learn/03-market-structure/05-multi-timeframe-analysis).

### Step 2: Mark the pool

Mark equal highs/lows or the area beyond a clear swing.

### Step 3: Wait for the sweep

Wait until price wicks beyond the pool. Do not guess in advance and enter.

### Step 4: Look at the body close

If the body comes back inside, the chance of a sweep goes up. Then wait for a CHOCH or BOS on the lower timeframe.

### Step 5: Keep the stop beyond the sweep

Keep the stop a little beyond the sweep's wick. If you keep the stop inside the wick, you will get swept again. Set the lot size with [position sizing](/en/learn/08-risk-management/02-position-sizing).

</Steps>

**Example (synthetic trade):** In the chart above, there were two equal lows near 2340. One candle dropped to 2334 and closed at 2345. The body was above the equal lows. After that, price broke the previous swing high and closed above it (BOS). You bought at 2352, with the stop below 2333 (the sweep's wick) and a target of 2378. The stop is far here, so the lot size must be kept small.

## Failed example: buying early because "this is a sweep" (synthetic/educational)

- **What could be seen:** price broke the equal lows and dropped. Someone assumed it was a sweep and bought right away.
- **Decision:** not waiting for the body close.
- **Result:** the candle closed below, and the next candles fell too. It was a real break. The stop was hit.
- **Lesson:** do not call a sweep from a wick. Wait until the body comes back inside.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                  | Why it happens                         | What to do                                 |
| :--------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------- |
| Thinking every wick is a sweep           | Assuming every break is fake           | Look at the body close and the next candle |
| Keeping the stop just below an equal low | The "clean" spot looks attractive      | Keep the stop beyond the sweep's wick      |
| Entering before the sweep                | Rushing                                | Enter after the sweep and confirmation     |
| Ignoring the higher timeframe            | Looking at only one part of the chart  | Check the bigger trend first               |
| Blaming everything on "big institutions" | Looking for an explanation for failure | Fix your own stop and risk plan            |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  Ask yourself: "Where are most traders putting their stops here?" If yours is
  there, move it one step. Moving the stop changes the risk-to-reward ratio, so
  recalculate the lot size too.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
  At the start of a session, price often touches the previous session's
  high/low. Keep those marked: [Trading
  Sessions](/en/learn/01-foundations/05-trading-sessions).
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **During big news:** price jumps, and sweeps and real breaks get mixed up.
- **In a strong trend:** price breaks the pool and does not come back, which is a real break.
- **On very small timeframes:** the noise shows many fake "sweeps".
- **When used alone:** a sweep does not tell you direction. It has to be matched with trend and structure.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- A liquidity pool is where stops and pending orders have built up.
- Pools are most common at equal highs/lows and beyond clear swings.
- Sweep: wick outside, body inside. Real break: body closes outside.
- Do not trade a sweep without checking the body close and the higher timeframe.
- Keep your own stop away from crowded places.

</KeyTakeaways>
