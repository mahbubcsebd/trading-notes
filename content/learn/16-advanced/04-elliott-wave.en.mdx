# Elliott Wave: Counting Rules, the Usable Part and the Limits

There is a common joke that if you show the same chart to ten Elliott followers, you will get twelve wave counts. There is truth in the joke: Elliott is subjective. Still, it has one part that is really useful: **a clear invalidation level**. This lesson centers on that.

## What you will be able to do

- **Recognize the five-wave impulse and the A-B-C correction.**
- **Check a count** with **three inviolable rules**.
- **Work out the stop and target** from a count.
- **Accept honestly the limit** of being subjective.

## Why it matters

- **A popular language for the wave shape of the market.** To follow other traders' talk you need to know the words.
- **Useful for risk.** The clarity of "if this price breaks, the count is wrong" gives the stop a reason.
- **A link to [Fibonacci](/en/learn/05-support-resistance/05-fibonacci-basics) and [trend structure](/en/learn/03-market-structure/02-trend-range-hhhl).**

Prerequisites: [Wyckoff Basics](/en/learn/16-advanced/03-wyckoff-basics) and [Swing High-Low](/en/learn/03-market-structure/01-swing-high-low).

## What is Elliott Wave

**Elliott Wave is a theory that says a trend advances in five waves (impulse) and is corrected in three waves (A-B-C correction).**

| Wave    | Direction                    | In short                            |
| :------ | :--------------------------- | :---------------------------------- |
| 1, 3, 5 | With the trend               | Impulse; 3 is usually the strongest |
| 2, 4    | Against the trend            | Correction                          |
| A, B, C | Correction after the impulse | A down, B up, C down                |

## Three inviolable rules (for the impulse)

1. **Wave 2 never goes past the starting point of wave 1** (it never retraces more than 100%).
2. **Wave 3 is never the shortest of 1, 3 and 5.**
3. **Wave 4 never enters the top of wave 1** (no overlap), in an ordinary impulse.

If any rule is broken, the count is wrong. This is the theory's own "test".

## Step by step: from a count to a trade

<Steps>

### Step 1: Find a clear wave 1

A clean push. If it is unclear, do not count.

### Step 2: Look at wave 2

Whether it went past the start of wave 1. If it did, the count is over.

### Step 3: Entry and stop

At the end of wave 2, or after its correction. The stop **just below** the start of wave 1, because that is the invalidation.

### Step 4: Target

At least 2R. A Fibonacci projection (such as multiples of the length of wave 1) is an extra guide, not a certainty.

### Step 5: If a rule breaks, get out at once

If wave 2 or wave 4 breaks a rule, the count is cancelled. Do not hold on saying "let me count another way".

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **What there was:** wave 1: 1.0800 to 1.0900. Wave 2 came down to 1.0850 (50% retraced), above the start.
- **While running:** buy 1.0853, stop 1.0795, target 1.0969 (2R).
- **Result:** wave 3 rose as far as 1.1010, the target was hit, +2R. Later waves 4, 5 and A-B-C formed.
- **Lesson:** from the count we got one clear stop. If the count is right, profit; if it is wrong, the loss is limited.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

- **What happened:** the same start, buy at 1.0853. But price pierced the start of wave 1 and fell.
- **Result:** the stop at 1.0795 was hit, -1R.
- **Why it failed:** wave 2 retraced more than 100% of wave 1, which breaks rule 1. The count was wrong at the root.
- **Lesson:** a wrong count is normal. Because there was a stop, the loss was held at 1R. With the mindset "let me count again", moving the stop would have increased the loss.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                | Why it happens             | What to do                           |
| :------------------------------------- | :------------------------- | :----------------------------------- |
| Placing a pretty count by looking back | Hindsight bias             | Wait live and then check the count   |
| Holding the count after a rule breaks  | Attachment to the idea     | Rule broken = get out                |
| Mixing several degrees at once         | Looking at every wave      | One timeframe, one count             |
| Treating a count as a forecast         | Wanting certainty          | Count = plan, not certainty          |
| Elliott with no stop                   | "I know where the wave is" | The stop just below the invalidation |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  Write an alternative count too: "if this is not wave 2, then what?" With two
  alternatives, the risk of sitting and believing in just one falls.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
  Before making Elliott your main strategy, run your own
  [backtest](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/01-how-to-backtest). Because it is
  subjective, results can differ by user. This writing is educational, not proof
  of effectiveness.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **If the waves are unclear:** if you count by force, everything fits.
- **On a small timeframe:** more noise, and both breaking and following the rules can be supported by reasoning.
- **In a complex correction:** flat, zigzag, triangle, double three and other classes take separate effort to learn.
- **On a news shock:** the count is useless in a moment.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- The impulse is five waves, the correction is three waves (A-B-C).
- The three rules: wave 2 does not pass the start, wave 3 is not the shortest, wave 4 does not enter wave 1.
- The real job of a count: a clear invalidation level and stop.
- A count is subjective; if a rule breaks, get out.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Practice

On an old D1 chart, mark an impulse and check the three rules. Then ask someone else (or yourself a week later) to count the same chart. How closely do the two counts match?
