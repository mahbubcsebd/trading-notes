# Automation: What to Know Before Running Rules in Code

Automation does not mean "the machine will make profit for me". The machine will run your rules **exactly**: if the rules are good, well; if they are wrong, every mistake, perfectly. This lesson says when automation makes sense, and which traps to avoid.

## What you will be able to do

- **Say which rules can be automated.**
- **Know the benefits and traps** of automation.
- **Understand the difference** between **in-sample and out-of-sample** testing.
- **Arrange the safe steps** before running live.

## Why it matters

- **Emotion is removed,** but the repetition of mistakes is added.
- **A natural step after [backtesting](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/01-how-to-backtest).** With automation, hundreds of trades are tested in minutes, and the risk of overfitting rises too.
- **Protection from sales traps.** To spot ads like "the robot gives 30% a month", you need basic ideas.

Prerequisites: [Performance Metrics and Forward Testing](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/02-performance-metrics-forward-testing).

## What is automation

**Automation means turning written rules into code and having a program look for signals or place trades.** Common forms: MetaTrader's Expert Advisor (EA), TradingView's Pine Script alert, your own script with Python.

| Level           | What it does                                       | Risk                    |
| :-------------- | :------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------- |
| Signal alert    | An alert when conditions match, you take the trade | Low                     |
| Semi-automatic  | Entry automatic, exit by you                       | Medium                  |
| Fully automatic | Entry, stop, exit all in code                      | High; needs supervision |

At the start, learn with a **signal alert**.

## Which rules can be automated

Rules that are **fully measurable**: every condition yes/no, nothing like "if it feels good". In [Entry, Exit and Risk Rules](/en/learn/14-trading-plan-system/02-entry-exit-risk-rules-ek-page-e) you learned to write such rules. What cannot be written there will not go into code either.

The rule worked in the trend and got hit in the range. The code **does not know** this; on every cross it does the same thing.

The rule in plain description (not code, pseudo):

```text
If the fast_average crosses above the slow_average and there is no position:
    buy
If the fast_average crosses below the slow_average and a position is open:
    close
(In the chart above, exit only on the cross; in reality you must add a separate stop and risk rules)
```

## Step by step: the path to automation

<Steps>

### Step 1: The rules on paper first

Complete, with numbers, like a [Plan](/en/learn/14-trading-plan-system/01-trading-plan-template).

### Step 2: Split the data

The first ~60–70% of the period is **in-sample** (building the rules), and the rest is **out-of-sample** (only for the final test). Do not change the rules before looking at the out-of-sample.

### Step 3: Count the costs

Spread, commission, slippage. If there are many trades, costs eat the profit.

### Step 4: The out-of-sample test

Run it once. If the result is bad, do not "tune and run again"; a new idea, new data.

### Step 5: Demo, then small live

Real execution (slippage, requote) is different from a backtest.

### Step 6: Supervision and the rules for closing

Do not relax because automation is running. Put the daily/weekly limits into the code itself ([stopping rules](/en/learn/14-trading-plan-system/05-kokhon-trading-bondho-korbo)).

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **What there was:** a simple MA cross rule, with the stop and risk fixed, a backtest with costs.
- **While running:** the rules were built on the in-sample and run once on the out-of-sample. The result was weaker than the in-sample, but in the same direction (positive).
- **Result:** started on demo at a small risk; after two months close to expectation.
- **Lesson:** it is normal for the out-of-sample result to be a bit weaker. If the direction is the same, confidence rises.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

<Chart
  id="automation-overfit-equity-01"
  height={420}
  caption="ACCOUNT, per trade (educational, imaginary example): the rules were tuned until the first 30 trades looked good. In the next 20 unseen trades, the curve gives back about 1,130 of the 1,782 profit."
/>

- **What happened:** the trader changed the parameters again and again (average length, stop, time) to make the in-sample curve look good.
- **Result:** from 10,000 to 11,782. But after the next 20 unseen trades, 10,653, a large part of the profit returned.
- **Why it failed:** the tuned rules had also memorized the noise of the past. This is overfitting.
- **Lesson:** the out-of-sample is the honest test. If you tune again after looking at it, that too becomes in-sample.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                              | Why it happens               | What to do                                   |
| :----------------------------------- | :--------------------------- | :------------------------------------------- |
| Tuning parameters again and again    | The greed for a pretty curve | Tune little, keep the out-of-sample separate |
| Backtesting without costs            | It seems simple              | Add spread/slippage                          |
| Believing the ad of a bought "robot" | Hoping for easy profit       | Not without your own data and testing        |
| Not supervising                      | "The machine is running it"  | Limits, alerts, a weekly review              |
| Going straight to big risk live      | Confidence in the backtest   | demo → small live                            |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  The simpler the rules, the lower the risk of overfitting. Few parameters, more
  logic. It is better to stay with two than to tune five.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
  Do not believe any claim like "sure profit", "risk-free", or "X% a month
  guaranteed". This is educational writing, not investment advice. Check any
  bought software independently first.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **If the rules are unclear:** they cannot be written in code.
- **When the type of market changes:** rules built for a trend will struggle in a range (the chart above).
- **On very little data:** with a few dozen trades the parameters mean nothing.
- **A gap in execution:** backtest prices and live prices differ, especially on news.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- Automation runs the rules exactly; it does not judge good or bad.
- Start with a signal alert.
- Split the data; look at the out-of-sample only once.
- Count the costs, use demo and small live, and always keep limits.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Practice

Write your simplest rule in pseudo-code (as above). Does the word "if it feels" or "good" appear in any line? If so, that rule is not yet ready for automation.
