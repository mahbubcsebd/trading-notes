# Volume Spread Analysis (VSA): Reading Strength and Weakness Bar by Bar

Wyckoff reads the story of a whole range. VSA reads **one sentence of that story at a time**. The question is simple: how much effort (volume) went into this one bar, and how does the result (spread and close) look? Do the two agree, or not?

## What you will learn

- Recognise the three parts of a bar: spread, close and volume.
- Read a bar with the effort-versus-result principle.
- Tell strength signals (stopping volume, shakeout, test) from weakness signals (no demand, upthrust).
- Understand why a single bar is not enough and **background** is needed.
- Know VSA's limits in Forex and Gold because of tick volume.

## History and origin

**Tom Williams** is the person most associated with naming and spreading VSA. From the 1990s he spread the method through books and training. His method came directly from **Wyckoff's ideas**, especially the effort-result principle.

- **Wyckoff** reads the whole range and its phases; **VSA** goes down to the level of each bar. So many people call VSA the "bar-level form" of Wyckoff.
- **Names and signals:** terms such as "no demand", "upthrust" and "stopping volume" are mostly used by teachers in the Wyckoff tradition. Not every teacher gives exactly the same definitions.
- Tom Williams's work is usually found in his own books and courses. For independent research, see the "Weaknesses" section.

Prerequisite: the Wyckoff Method (previous lesson). Its effort-result idea is the main foundation here.

## Core philosophy

**VSA's question: do this bar's spread, close and volume show strength or weakness?**

The belief is this: the work of big players leaves a mark on volume. So if you compare volume and price movement in every bar, you can see which way the big hand is leaning.

An analogy: a crowd at a shop's cash counter (volume), yet very few sales (small spread). Something is off: people are coming but not buying, or someone inside is turning everyone away.

<Callout type="note">
  VSA is a **bar-based interpretation**. It does not say "a big player is doing
  this"; it says, "this bar behaves like strength / like weakness." The
  interpretation is an inference, not proof.
</Callout>

## Core concepts

### 1. Three components: spread, close, volume

- **Spread:** the distance from the bar's high to its low. Wide means a big move.
- **Close:** where the bar closes: near the high, in the middle, or near the low.
- **Volume:** how much activity in that bar. More, less or normal compared with the previous few bars.

The three are read **together**, not separately. Wide spread + close near the high + high volume looks like strength. Wide spread + close near the low + high volume hints at the opposite.

### 2. Effort vs result

**Volume is the effort, spread is the result. If the two agree, the bar is normal; if not, that is the signal.**

- Big volume, small spread: much effort, little result. Someone on the other side is absorbing.
- Small volume, big spread: price moved without effort. A hollow move that will not last.

An analogy: two people pushing a heavy cupboard, not pulling it with a string. If the cupboard barely moves, someone on the other side is pushing back.

### 3. Strength signals

A few bar types usually hint at demand or exhausted selling:

| Signal          | What it looks like                                               | Usual reading                        |
| :-------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------- |
| Stopping volume | Very big volume in a downtrend; the fall stops / the close lifts | Big buyers are absorbing the selling |
| Shakeout        | A new low on very high volume, but closing near the high         | Weak hands have left                 |
| Test            | A narrow bar after the shakeout, on the lowest volume            | Supply has dried up                  |
| No supply       | A down bar, narrow spread, low volume                            | Sellers are not interested           |

**Shakeout and Test:** a strong pair. First scare the weak out (high volume), then check whether any sellers remain (low volume). This is the bar-level form of Wyckoff's spring and test.

### 4. Weakness signals

The opposite side:

| Signal         | What it looks like                                                            | Usual reading                             |
| :------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------- |
| No demand      | An up bar, narrow spread, volume equal to or lower than the previous two bars | Buyers not interested, the rise is hollow |
| Upthrust       | Touches a new high and closes near the low, big volume                        | A trap above: buyers get stuck            |
| Buying climax  | Very big volume, wide up bar, large upper wick                                | Demand at its end                         |
| Effort to rise | High volume but a small up spread                                             | Sellers above are absorbing               |

**Upthrust:** a related idea is a [liquidity sweep](/en/learn/03-market-structure/04-liquidity-stop-hunts). Price touches the stops above and returns.

### 5. Background: one bar is not enough

**A bar's meaning depends on what happened before it.** The same "small spread, low volume up bar" means nothing in a pause inside a strong uptrend, but if the previous few bars showed weakness, it is no demand.

An analogy: a student keeping quiet in class. Before an exam it means one thing, the day before a holiday another.

The rule: **background first, then the signal bar.** Background means: where (range edge, a new high/low), how the earlier trend looked, and how volume has been over the last few bars.

### 6. Comparing volume: what is "high" and what is "low"?

In VSA, volume is always **relative**: against the average of the previous 10–20 bars. The number alone says nothing. So "huge" usually means one and a half to two times the average or more, and "low" means half or less. This limit is not a strict rule, just a rough guide.

### 7. The tick volume problem

<Callout type="warning">
  **The limit of volume in Forex and Gold.** Spot Forex and spot Gold have no
  central exchange. The volume in MT4/MT5 is really **tick volume**: how many
  times price changed on your broker's server. It is not actual trading. **VSA
  is hit hardest by this limit,** because each of its signals depends on subtle
  differences in volume. An alternative: Gold futures (CME GC) volume. And watch
  for big differences (a sudden doubling or halving), not small ones.
</Callout>

<Steps>

### Step 1: Stopping volume

At the end of the downtrend one bar touches the low **2296** and closes at 2306. Volume **3200**, almost three times the average. So much selling, yet price did not fall further. Big buyers are absorbing: stopping volume.

### Step 2: Spring and Test

Later a bar drops to **2287** and closes at 2304 (volume 1500): a new low but the close has come back up, like a shakeout. Two bars later a narrow bar (spread 12) with volume only **450**, about two fifths of the average: the test. There are almost no sellers.

### Step 3: The wide up bar of strength

Price breaks the range ceiling and closes at 2346. Spread 17 (above average), volume **2400**, close near the high. Effort and result agree: strength.

### Step 4: No demand

High up, in the 2458–2474 area, a few small up bars: spread 8, volume about **600**, half the average. There is a rise, but no one is buying. No demand.

### Step 5: Upthrust

The next bar touches a high of **2487** but closes at **2467**, near the low (spread 22, the widest on the chart). Volume **2600**. Big volume and a weak close at a new high: an upthrust. Price then falls.

</Steps>

<Callout type="tip">
  Look at the order of steps 4 and 5: first a hollow rise on low volume (the
  background is built), then the signal bar. The same upthrust-like bar in the
  middle of a strong trend would not mean as much.
</Callout>

## Basic trade logic

In VSA the signal bar is usually the **setup**; the entry comes on the confirmation of the next bar.

| Part       | Rule (common form)                                             | On the shared chart (synthetic)           |
| :--------- | :------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------- |
| Background | Under a downtrend/range, signs of absorption (stopping volume) | Under the range at 2296                   |
| Setup      | A shakeout and then a low-volume test                          | The 2287 spring, then the volume-450 test |
| Entry      | Above the high of the test bar (demand returning)              | About 2314                                |
| Stop loss  | Below the shakeout low                                         | 2285                                      |
| Target     | The next level or a multiple of the stop distance (e.g. 2R)    | 2314 + 58 = 2372                          |

The arithmetic: risk 2314 − 2285 = 29, reward 2372 − 2314 = 58, ratio **1:2**. On this synthetic chart price later passed 2372.

## Strengths

- **Fine-grained view.** Even one bar offers clues; you need not wait for a whole range.
- **Complements Wyckoff.** Wyckoff tells the story, VSA looks for the meaning of each sentence.
- **Clear stop logic.** The shakeout low or the upthrust high: you know where the signal is wrong.
- **The effort-result principle is universal.** The question "does volume agree with price?" helps in any method.

## Weaknesses and limits

- **A single bar without background is weak.** The exact same bar can mean strength or weakness, depending on context.
- **Judgment-based (subjectivity: medium).** How much is "high volume", how narrow is a "narrow spread", how close to the low is "near the low": definitions differ between teachers. There are rough rules, but not mechanical ones.
- **Most dependent on tick volume.** Spot Forex and Gold have no actual volume. Tick volume changes when you change broker. For a method whose every signal depends on volume, this is the biggest problem.
- **Easy to arrange after the fact.** Going back over a chart and saying "that was no demand" is easy. Live, many bars are ambiguous.
- **More signals, less certainty.** If you try to interpret every bar you can always find a "signal". Not all signals matter.

**Evidence status:** there is little independent, large-scale research on VSA; most of the knowledge comes from Tom Williams's books and practitioners' courses. General research on technical analysis is mixed (Park and Irwin, 2007). **No method guarantees profit.** Verify it in your own backtest.

## Combining with other methods

- **VSA + Wyckoff:** the most natural. Wyckoff says "which phase now", VSA says "what is this bar saying". See the previous lesson.
- **VSA + Price Action:** at support/resistance a VSA signal means more, because there is background.
- **VSA + Volume Profile:** knowing at which prices more trading happened makes "a rise on low volume" clearer. See the next lesson.

**What not to mix:** a pile of volume oscillators with VSA. Both are built from the same raw information, so certainty does not grow. Look at volume and VWAP indicators separately: [Volume and VWAP](/en/learn/07-technical-indicators/06-volume-vwap).

## How to start

<Steps>

### Step 1: A chart with volume

Open XAUUSD H4 and turn volume on. Know that it is tick volume.

### Step 2: Write the three components

For each of the last 50 bars write: spread (wide/medium/narrow), close (top/middle/bottom), volume (high/normal/low).

### Step 3: Find the unusual ones

Mark the bars where effort and result did not agree (big volume, small spread; or the reverse).

### Step 4: See what happened next

For each unusual bar, write the next 5 bars. How often did the signal work? What was the background?

### Step 5: Write the rules and backtest

Pick one pair (such as shakeout + test) and write the rules. [Backtest](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/01-how-to-backtest) on at least 30 examples.

</Steps>

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                    | Why it is a problem                | Fix                                                   |
| :----------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------- |
| Trading a single bar without background    | The same bar can have two meanings | Context first: where, what trend, how volume has been |
| Treating tick volume as real volume        | Differs by broker, approximate     | Watch only big differences; look at futures volume    |
| Treating every bar as a signal             | You can always "find" something    | Only at key places (range edges, new highs/lows)      |
| Reading a volume number without comparison | A number alone says nothing        | Compare with the average of the previous 10–20 bars   |
| Entering without confirmation              | One bar can reverse on the next    | Enter on the next bar's strength, and set a stop      |

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- VSA reads strength or weakness from each bar's spread, close and volume together.
- The core principle: when effort (volume) and result (spread) disagree, that is the signal.
- Strength: stopping volume, shakeout, test, no supply. Weakness: no demand, upthrust, buying climax.
- One bar is not enough; background first, then the signal bar.
- Tick volume in spot Forex/Gold is approximate, and this method is hit hardest. No guaranteed profit.

</KeyTakeaways>
