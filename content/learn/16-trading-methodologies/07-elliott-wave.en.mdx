# Elliott Wave: Reading the Market by Counting the Crowd's Waves

Have you watched ocean waves? A few big waves roll in, then pull back a little, then advance again. Elliott says the market is the same: the hopes and fears of the crowd make waves in a set rhythm. The question is: which step of the wave are we on?

## What you will learn

- State Elliott Wave's core idea: five impulse waves and three corrective waves.
- Understand the difference between **three inviolable rules** and **guidelines**.
- Know where Fibonacci ratios come in.
- Work out when a count is invalidated.
- Understand why one chart can hold several valid counts.

## History and origin

- **Ralph Nelson Elliott** was an accountant. In the 1930s, looking at many years of stock index data, he noticed a repeating rhythm in price movement. He called it the **Wave Principle**.
- **Frost and Prechter:** in 1978 A.J. Frost and Robert Prechter popularised the idea again in the book _Elliott Wave Principle_. Today's rules and labels come mostly from there.
- **Differences between teachers.** Teachers differ slightly on rules, guidelines and labels. This lesson uses one common version.

## Core philosophy

**Elliott's question: which step of the crowd's wave are we in, and where will my count be proven wrong?**

The idea is this: market movement expresses the collective psychology of people. Hope rises, joy arrives, then fear and despair. This cycle shows up on the chart as a repeating shape: five steps with the trend, then three against it. And waves appear in the same shape at every scale (fractal).

An analogy: the crowd at a village fair. First a few people (wave 1), some go back (wave 2), then word spreads and a huge crowd arrives (wave 3), it thins a little (wave 4), the last enthusiasts turn up (wave 5). Then the fair breaks up.

<Callout type="warning">
  This is an **explanatory framework**, not a proven law of nature. Counting
  waves is always somewhat a matter of judgment, and often it is clear only
  afterwards which count was right.
</Callout>

## Core concepts

### 1. Impulse: five steps

**An impulse (motive) wave moves with the trend, in five parts: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.** Three (1, 3, 5) go with the trend, two (2, 4) are short pauses against it.

- **Wave 1:** the first move; many still doubt it.
- **Wave 2:** a pullback that takes back part of wave 1.
- **Wave 3:** usually the strongest and longest; the crowd joins.
- **Wave 4:** a pause or pullback.
- **Wave 5:** the last push, often with fading momentum.

### 2. Correction: three steps, A-B-C

**After an impulse a correction against it usually follows, in the common form A-B-C.** A and C go against, B is a small return in the middle.

Corrections come in several shapes (zigzag, flat, triangle). They are harder to recognise than impulses, and this is where most mistakes happen. This lesson takes only the simple A-B-C.

### 3. The three inviolable rules

Three rules for counting an impulse. Break even one and the count is wrong; discard it and count again:

1. **Wave 2 never goes beyond the start of wave 1.** That is, it never retraces more than 100% of wave 1.
2. **Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5.** (It need not be the longest, but it is never the shortest.)
3. **Wave 4 never enters wave 1's price territory.** (In a standard impulse; a special shape called a diagonal is an exception.)

These three make Elliott somewhat **testable**: break a rule and the count is dead, no explanation allowed. This is the method's biggest practical strength.

### 4. Guidelines and Fibonacci

Beyond the rules there are **guidelines**: tendencies, but not strict. Most involve [Fibonacci ratios](/en/learn/05-support-resistance/05-fibonacci-basics):

| Step        | Common guideline                                              |
| :---------- | :------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Wave 2      | Retraces about 50%–61.8% of wave 1                            |
| Wave 3      | About 1.618 times wave 1 (or 2.618 times)                     |
| Wave 4      | Retraces about 23.6%–38.2% of wave 3                          |
| Wave 5      | Equal to wave 1, or about 0.618 times waves 1–3               |
| Alternation | Waves 2 and 4 tend to differ in form (one sharp, one complex) |

**Disagreement:** these numbers differ a little between teachers, and they are tendencies. If a guideline fails you cannot call the count wrong; if a rule fails you can.

### 5. Degree: big and small waves

**Waves within waves.** Break down a big wave 3 and it contains its own five small steps. Elliott gave names to these scales (from Grand Supercycle to Subminuette). The practical meaning: decide first which scale of wave you are counting on your timeframe.

An analogy: maps. A country map, a district map, a village map: the same shape, different scales.

### 6. Invalidation: when the count is dead

**Every count has an invalidation price.** If you buy at the end of wave 2, the invalidation price is the start of wave 1 (rule 1). Go below it and the count is wrong, and so is the trade.

This is what makes Elliott truly useful: "I believe this is the start of wave 3; if I am wrong, it will be at exactly this price."

### 7. More than one valid count

**More than one valid count is possible on the same chart.** Teachers call this an "alternate count". If the rules are met, two different counts can both stand, and which is right usually becomes clear only later.

This is why subjectivity is "high": there are rules, but many counts can obey them.

<Steps>

### Step 1: Where does it start?

Wave 1 starts at the spring low of **2287**. Choosing this point is itself a judgment: someone else would have made one bigger count from the end of the earlier downtrend.

### Step 2: Waves 1 and 2

Wave 1: from 2287 to about **2365** (length 78). Wave 2: down to **2336** (length 29, about 37% of wave 1). Rule 1 is met (well above 2287). But it is shallower than the guideline's 50%–61.8%.

### Step 3: Wave 3

From 2336 it rises to **2454** (length 118, about 1.5 times wave 1). Close to 1.618, not exact. Wave 3 is now the longest: rule 2 holds.

### Step 4: Wave 4

Down to **2418** (about 30% of wave 3). 2418 > wave 1's high (2365): rule 3 holds, no overlap.

### Step 5: Wave 5 and the end

From 2418 it rises to **2474** (length 56), smaller than wave 3 (rule 2 holds). The next candle touches 2487 and returns. Whether you end wave 5 at 2474 or 2487 is also a judgment: ending at 2487 gives wave 5 a length of 69, still smaller than wave 3. Two different counts, both obeying the rules.

### Step 6: A-B-C

After the top price has fallen to 2394. This could be the start of A-B-C, but it is not complete yet. Elliott's honest answer: "A may be under way; B and C have not come."

</Steps>

<Callout type="tip">
  Note that all three rules held in this count. But the guidelines fit only
  partly (wave 2 shallow, wave 3 about 1.5 times). This is normal: the rules
  pass the test, the guidelines stay "nearby".
</Callout>

## Basic trade logic

An Elliott trade usually enters at the end of wave 2 for wave 3, because wave 3 is usually the biggest and the invalidation price (the start of wave 1) is clear.

| Part      | Rule (common form)                                       | On the shared chart (synthetic) |
| :-------- | :------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------ |
| Setup     | Wave 1 complete, wave 2 seems to have ended              | Wave 2 low 2336                 |
| Entry     | After confirmation of the turn at the end of wave 2      | About 2347                      |
| Stop loss | Below the start of wave 1 (the price that breaks rule 1) | 2285                            |
| Target    | Wave 3 = 1.618 times wave 1, from the end of wave 2      | 2336 + 126 = 2462               |

The arithmetic: risk 2347 − 2285 = 62, reward 2462 − 2347 = 115, a ratio of about **1:1.85**. On this synthetic chart price rose to 2454 and did not touch 2462. So with a target order the trade would still be open, and after the top, when price falls to 2394, the result would stand at about +47 (still above entry). The honest truth: you cannot know exactly where wave 3 will end.

## Strengths

- **Gives the big picture.** A framework for which stage of the trend you are in.
- **Clear invalidation.** Break any of the three rules and the count is dead; stop logic is simple.
- **Targets with Fibonacci.** Targets and retracement areas can be calculated in advance.
- **Applies at every scale.** The same language from H4 to W1.

## Weaknesses and limits

- **High subjectivity.** More than one valid count on the same chart. The starting point, the end of wave 5 and the shape of the correction are all judgments (see steps 1 and 5 above).
- **Counting afterwards is easy.** Placing waves on an old chart is simple; live, it is hard to know which wave is under way.
- **Corrections are hard.** Zigzag, flat, triangle, combination: many shapes, hard to recognise.
- **The "invalidated, new count" trap.** If you keep changing the count, the method is never proven wrong. This is a serious criticism of the method: when it is wrong, another count can always be found.
- **Guidelines fit only partly.** Fibonacci ratios are often "nearby"; how near is acceptable is not fixed.

**Evidence status:** independent, large-scale research on Elliott Wave is limited, and strict testing is hard because the definition of a count is itself a judgment. General research on technical analysis is mixed (Park and Irwin, 2007). **No method guarantees profit.**

## Who it suits

| Suits you if you…                             | Does not suit you if you…                   |
| :-------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------ |
| Like the big picture and structure            | Want one clear answer, not alternate counts |
| Can tolerate more than one interpretation     | Get unsettled by uncertainty                |
| Work slowly on H4/D1/W1                       | Scalp on M1–M15                             |
| Have learned Price Action and Fibonacci first | Are a complete beginner                     |

## Combining with other methods

- **Elliott + Price Action:** swings and structure are the base for counting waves. If Price Action is not solid, Elliott floats.
- **Elliott + Harmonic:** both use Fibonacci; Elliott measures wave lengths, Harmonic the points of a pattern. See the next lesson.
- **Elliott + RSI divergence:** momentum fades in wave 5, so divergence often coincides with wave 5. A supporting signal, not proof.
- **Elliott + Wyckoff:** Wyckoff's spring and SOS often line up with the start of wave 1 and with wave 3. A useful pair for the big picture.

**What not to mix:** ten indicators over an Elliott count so that you can match them and prove the count right. That is confirmation bias.

## How to start

<Steps>

### Step 1: Memorise the three rules

Write rules 1, 2 and 3 in your own words. Check them first in every count.

### Step 2: 20 impulses on old charts

Find 20 clean impulses on XAUUSD D1. How many pass the three rules?

### Step 3: Count first, then look, live

Cover the right side of the chart and count only up to wave 1-2. Then uncover the rest: did your count hold?

### Step 4: Write an alternate count

With every count write an alternate. Are the two invalidation prices different?

### Step 5: Write the rule and backtest

Write only the rule "buy at the end of wave 2, stop below the start of wave 1" and [backtest](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/01-how-to-backtest) at least 30 examples. Do the counting by a fixed rule (such as a swing distance), not by your own judgment.

</Steps>

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                      | Why it is a problem            | Fix                                                          |
| :------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Continuing a count that broke a rule         | A broken rule kills the count  | Check the three rules every time                             |
| Treating a guideline as a rule               | Fibonacci is only a tendency   | Keep rules and guidelines separate                           |
| Believing "the count is fine" without a stop | A wrong count means a big loss | A stop at the invalidation price                             |
| Changing the count again and again           | It is never proven wrong       | Before each trade write one count and its invalidation price |
| Looking at only one count                    | Ignoring the alternate count   | Write the alternate count too                                |

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- Elliott's structure: five waves with the trend (impulse), three against it (A-B-C).
- Three inviolable rules: wave 2 not beyond 100%, wave 3 not the shortest, wave 4 not into wave 1.
- Fibonacci ratios are guidelines, not rules.
- Every count has an invalidation price; that is the stop.
- Subjectivity is high: more than one valid count on one chart. No guaranteed profit.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Quiz

1. How many waves make up an impulse and a correction?
2. What are the three inviolable rules?
3. What is the difference between a rule and a guideline?
4. Where is the stop if you buy at the end of wave 2?
5. Why is more than one count possible on the same chart?

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1.** An impulse has 5 (1-2-3-4-5); a correction usually has 3 (A-B-C).

**2.** Wave 2 never goes beyond the start of wave 1; wave 3 is never the shortest of 1, 3 and 5; wave 4 never enters wave 1's territory.

**3.** Breaking a rule kills the count; a guideline (such as a Fibonacci ratio) is only a tendency, and missing it does not kill the count.

**4.** Below the start of wave 1, because going there breaks rule 1 and the count is invalid.

**5.** The starting point, the end of a wave and the shape of a correction are judgments; several different counts can obey the three rules.

</details>
