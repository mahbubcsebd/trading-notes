# Market Participants: Who Trades in the Forex Market

Price does not move on its own. Behind every move, someone is buying or selling. Once you know who they are, why they trade and how big they are, a lot of chart behaviour becomes easier to explain.

## What you will be able to do

- **Recognise** the main groups of participants.
- **Tell apart** what each one is trying to do.
- **Understand** the two broker models.
- **Get a sense** of how big players affect price behaviour.

## Why it matters

- **Not everyone trades for profit.** Corporations trade to avoid risk, and central banks trade for policy reasons. So price is not always "logical".
- **Retail traders are small.** Knowing this teaches both humility and realism.
- **It is the base for understanding how [news](/en/learn/11-fundamental-intermarket-analysis/06-news-trade-avoid-korbo) moves the market.**

Prerequisites: [What Is Forex](/en/learn/01-foundations/01-what-is-forex).

## Who the participants are

**Market participants are the people and institutions whose buying and selling, taken together, set prices in the Forex market.**

| Group                           | Goal                                                    | Effect on price                          |
| :------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------ | :--------------------------------------- |
| Central banks                   | Interest rates, monetary policy, sometimes intervention | Lasting impact from policy announcements |
| Commercial and investment banks | Client orders, own trading, price-making                | The centre of the interbank market       |
| Corporations                    | Hedging the currency risk of imports and exports        | Large orders, not aimed at profit        |
| Funds (hedge/managed)           | Profit, portfolio rebalancing                           | Large speculative positions              |
| Retail traders                  | Personal profit                                         | Tiny alone, can be noticeable together   |

Exact percentages for each group are not given here because they change; see the BIS survey.

## Brokers and market layers

Retail traders do not go to the interbank market directly. A **broker** sits in between.

| Model                       | How it works                                              | What to watch for                               |
| :-------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- |
| Market maker (dealing desk) | The broker takes the other side and sets the price itself | Possible conflict of interest; check regulation |
| STP/ECN                     | The broker passes your order on to liquidity providers    | May charge commission or wider spreads          |

Neither model is good or bad in itself. More detail: [How to Choose a Broker](/en/learn/02-broker-platform-setup/01-broker-kivabe-choose-korbo).

## Step by step: the habit of asking who is behind the price

<Steps>

### Step 1: Check the schedule of big events

Central bank meetings, inflation data. Check the [calendar](/en/learn/11-fundamental-intermarket-analysis/02-economic-calendar-nfp-cpi-fomc) in advance.

### Step 2: Ask: who wants what?

Is this move driven by a policy announcement, or by routine buying and selling?

### Step 3: Don't chase big moves

When a big player's order pushes price and is done, following it is risky.

### Step 4: Remember your own size

Your order does not move the market. So it is not "the market is on my side" but "I am going with the market".

### Step 5: Know how your broker works too

If you know how execution and prices reach you, there are fewer unpleasant surprises.

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **What was known:** A major central bank announces its interest rate today.
- **Action:** The trader did not trade before or during the announcement. Once the direction was clear afterwards, they joined with small risk.
- **Result:** No loss from the spike; a small profit from the slower move that followed.
- **Lesson:** If you know who the big players are and when their decisions come out, you can avoid risk.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

- **What happened:** The trader traded right at the data release with a tight stop. The first spike took the stop, then price reversed.
- **Why it failed:** Big players' orders and widening spreads made the stop useless.
- **Lesson:** At the moment of a big event, retail traders have little edge. Waiting is also a decision.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                   | Why it happens                    | What to do                       |
| :---------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| Assuming "big players hunt my stop"       | No proof, but a convenient excuse | Look for your own mistakes first |
| Explaining every move by the central bank | Looking for a simple cause        | Keep several causes in mind      |
| Not checking the broker                   | Going by the ads                  | Read its regulation and policies |
| Thinking you are bigger than the market   | Overconfidence                    | Respect size and limits          |
| Trading at the moment of the news         | Excitement                        | Rules first, news later          |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  Make it a rule to wait 30 minutes after every major announcement. Once the
  first spike settles, the real direction is easier to see.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
  Claims like "banks deliberately make retail traders lose" cannot be verified.
  What you can rely on is your own risk control.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **On small timeframes:** You cannot know whose order it is; you can only see the timing of events.
- **When you oversimplify:** Thousands of causes act on the market at once.
- **With an unknown broker:** Not knowing its model and regulation is a risk.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- Five groups: central banks, banks, corporations, funds, retail traders.
- Not everyone is trading for profit.
- Brokers: market maker or STP/ECN.
- At the moment of a big event, retail traders have little edge.

</KeyTakeaways>
