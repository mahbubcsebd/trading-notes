# Central Bank, Interest Rates and Inflation: Why a Currency Moves

The news said "interest rates unchanged", and price jumped 50 pips. Or rates went up, yet the currency fell. This is because the market reacts to the **gap in expectations**, more than to the event itself.

## What you will be able to do

- **Say what** a central bank controls with interest rates.
- **Tell the difference** between the words **hawkish and dovish**.
- **Explain why the market may not move** on an expected decision.

## Why it matters

- **A lot of a currency's long-term direction comes from here.** Behind a technical trend there is often an interest rate story.
- **You can spot the moment of a big spike.** If you know the day of the decision, you can understand the risk in advance.
- **If you chase the headline, you go the wrong way.** If you understand expectations, you are surprised less.

Prerequisites: [Market Participants](/en/learn/01-foundations/02-market-participants).

## The main idea

**A central bank is the main regulator of a country's currency and financial system (for example the Federal Reserve in the US, the ECB in the eurozone). Its main tool is the interest rate.**

Think of a water tap. High inflation means the economy is overheating, so the central bank raises rates and squeezes the tap. When the economy is weak, it cuts rates and opens the tap.

| Term      | Meaning                                     | Usual effect on the currency |
| :-------- | :------------------------------------------ | :--------------------------- |
| Hawkish   | A lean toward raising or keeping rates high | Tends to get stronger        |
| Dovish    | A lean toward cutting or keeping rates soft | Tends to get weaker          |
| Inflation | The rate at which prices rise               | Drives the rate decision     |
| Rate hike | Raising the interest rate                   | Usually hawkish              |
| Rate cut  | Cutting the interest rate                   | Usually dovish               |

The reason: holding a currency at a high rate gives a higher return, so investors lean toward that currency. This is a tendency, not a rule.

## The expectation gap

The market puts its guess about a decision into the price before the decision. So the move comes from the **gap between expectation and reality**.

| Situation                       | Likely reaction                              |
| :------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------- |
| Tougher than expected (hawkish) | The currency gets stronger                   |
| Softer than expected (dovish)   | The currency gets weaker                     |
| Exactly as expected             | A small move, or a spike and then a reversal |

## Step by step: how to read a decision day

<Steps>

### Step 1: Know the expectation

On the calendar, look at the "forecast" and "previous" values. What the market expects is the base.

### Step 2: Compare the result

See whether the actual value is tougher or softer than the forecast.

### Step 3: Look at the structure that follows, not the first candle

At the moment of the announcement, the spread and the spike are at their largest. Waiting for a [retest](/en/learn/11-fundamental-intermarket-analysis/06-news-trade-avoid-korbo) is safer.

### Step 4: Place it in the bigger picture

Look at the direction of policy (steadily hawkish or dovish), not one decision.

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **Known in advance:** the market expects rates to stay unchanged.
- **Result:** the decision and the message are tougher than expected, the USD jumps, EURUSD falls about 55 pips and keeps falling in the following hours.
- **Lesson:** when the expectation gap and the slow move afterward go the same way, the direction is a reliable context.

- **What was visible:** thinking "a hawkish decision means the USD rises", a rush to sell EURUSD at the moment of the decision, or to buy the other way.
- **Result:** on the expected decision, price first runs up and then reverses, and the hurried entry hits the stop.
- **Why it failed:** the news was already in the price, and there was nothing new.
- **Lesson:** do not look at "good or bad news". Look at "better or worse than expected".

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                      | Why it happens                      | What to do                             |
| :------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------- | :------------------------------------- |
| Taking the direction from the headline       | "Rates rose, so the currency rises" | Compare the actual with the forecast   |
| Treating one decision as final               | Liking an easy story                | Look at the steady direction of policy |
| Thinking of inflation and rates as separate  | Not knowing the link                | Inflation drives the rate decision     |
| Entering right at the moment of the decision | Fear of missing out                 | Wait for structure after the spike     |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  A central bank does not say directly what it will do, but the tone of its
  statements changes. Words like "cautious", "patient" or "further action if
  needed" show a hawkish or dovish lean.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
  The examples here are simplified. In reality, growth, employment and
  geopolitics work together with interest rates.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **When several news items come together:** you cannot separate which one moved price.
- **On a short timeframe:** on M1-M5 a spike is like noise, and the story is hard to read.
- **When it was already priced in:** the decision brings no big move.
- **When other causes weigh more on the currency:** in a war or a crisis, interest rate math is pushed aside.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- A central bank uses interest rates to control inflation and the economy.
- Hawkish usually strengthens a currency and dovish weakens it.
- Price moves on the gap between expectation and reality, not on the headline.
- On an expected decision, a spike can reverse.

</KeyTakeaways>
