# Currency Strength and DXY: How to Read the Strength of the Dollar

EURUSD is rising. The question: is the EUR getting stronger, or the USD getting weaker? The answer changes the context of the trade. The DXY is a quick answer to that question.

## What you will be able to do

- **Say what the DXY is and what it is made of.**
- **Understand the relationship** between EURUSD and the DXY.
- **Recognize when the relationship breaks**, and be careful.

## Why it matters

- **A pair has two currencies.** When price moves, you need to know which currency is causing it.
- **The dollar is part of almost everything.** EURUSD, GBPUSD, USDJPY, XAUUSD, all of them.
- **You get context fast.** One chart shows the overall state of the dollar.

Prerequisites: [Economic Calendar](/en/learn/11-fundamental-intermarket-analysis/02-economic-calendar-nfp-cpi-fomc).

## What is the DXY

**The DXY (US Dollar Index) is an index that measures the average strength of the USD against a basket of six major currencies. The basket: EUR, JPY, GBP, CAD, SEK and CHF.**

The EUR is the biggest part of the basket. So the DXY and EURUSD usually move in opposite directions. A rising DXY means the USD is stronger overall, and a falling one means it is weaker. On your broker the index may be named DXY, USDX or something else.

| DXY  | USD    | EURUSD usually | USDJPY usually |
| :--- | :----- | :------------- | :------------- |
| Up   | Strong | Down           | Up             |
| Down | Weak   | Up             | Down           |

Notice: in a pair with the USD written first (USDJPY) the direction matches, and in a pair with it last (EURUSD) it is opposite.

## Step by step: how to use the DXY

<Steps>

### Step 1: Look at the bigger direction of the DXY

Which way the DXY goes on H4 or Daily is the main context.

### Step 2: Match it with the direction of your pair

If you are thinking of buying EURUSD, see whether the DXY is falling.

### Step 3: When they agree, raise your confidence

When both charts tell the same story, the setup has a strong context.

### Step 4: When they disagree, be careful

A disagreement means either another currency is at work, or the relationship is breaking. Cut the size or wait.

### Step 5: Context only, not a signal

The entry comes from your own [setup](/en/learn/09-price-action-entries-strategies/01-breakout-retest-pullback-entry).

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **Set in advance:** the H1 setup is a EURUSD buy, and I will look at the direction of the DXY.
- **Result:** the DXY kept falling, and after a pullback EURUSD went to a new high. Since both charts told the same story, you held with confidence.
- **Lesson:** the DXY supplies context. It does not make your decision.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

/>

- **What was visible:** thinking "the DXY is rising, so EURUSD will fall this time", a sell.
- **Result:** EURUSD kept rising and the sell hit the stop.
- **Why it failed:** the inverse relationship is a tendency. Other currencies in the basket (such as JPY or GBP) were keeping the DXY up.
- **Lesson:** when the relationship breaks, do not use it as a signal. The price's own structure comes first.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                              | Why it happens                | What to do                                     |
| :----------------------------------- | :---------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------- |
| Treating the DXY as an entry signal  | Looking for an easy rule      | Use it only as context                         |
| Looking only at the M5 DXY           | Misreading noise              | First look at the bigger direction on H1 or H4 |
| Treating the relationship as certain | It matched in the past        | Check the match every time                     |
| Using the same rule on every pair    | Forgetting where the USD sits | See whether the USD is first or last           |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  Look at another option: if two USD pairs (such as EURUSD and GBPUSD) go the
  same way together, the USD is probably the main driver. If they go different
  ways, the cause is more likely another currency.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
  Taking the same USD bet on two pairs at once doubles the risk. Remember
  [Correlation risk](/en/learn/08-risk-management/06-correlation-risk).
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **On EUR-specific news:** EURUSD moves only on eurozone news, and the other parts of the DXY do not move.
- **On JPY's or GBP's own news:** the DXY misleads because a part of the basket moves.
- **On a very short timeframe:** in minute-level noise the relationship cannot be seen.
- **When indices differ between brokers:** match the name and the makeup.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- The DXY is the average strength of the USD against a basket of six currencies.
- The EUR is the biggest part, so EURUSD usually moves opposite to the DXY.
- The DXY is context, not an entry signal.
- The relationship can break, so check it every time.

</KeyTakeaways>
