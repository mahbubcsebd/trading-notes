# Book Notes: Turning Notes From Books Into Testable Rules

After reading a book, many lines are marked with a highlighter, and three months later nothing is remembered. The goal of a book is not knowledge. It is **one tested change in your plan**. This lesson is the template for that path.

## What you will be able to do

- **Write the claims** from a book separately.
- **Decide the way to test** each claim.
- **Arrange your own reading list.**

## Why it matters

- **The author's experience is not your proof.** What worked for them may not work in your market, time and temperament.
- **Without notes, reading is lost.** And highlights alone are no use.
- **Supply for a [Backtest](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/01-how-to-backtest).** Every testable claim is a small test.

Prerequisites: [Building a Plan](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/05-building-a-plan).

## What is a book note

**A book note is writing one claim of a book in your own words and adding the way to test it and the verdict on the result.**

| Column        | What to write                                                    |
| :------------ | :--------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Book, chapter | the name, the page (to find it later)                            |
| Claim         | what the author says, in your own words, in one or two sentences |
| Type          | fact / attitude / testable rule                                  |
| Test          | how will I check it on my own data? (if testable)                |
| Verdict       | not tested / did not work / worked / added to the plan           |

**Three kinds of claims:** (1) a fact (such as the definition of a term), (2) an attitude (such as "treat a loss as a cost"), (3) a testable rule (such as "buy when the 20-day high breaks"). Only the third is a subject for a backtest.

## A suggested reading list

The books below are much discussed in trading education. The subject of each book is brief; you will read and check the specific claims inside yourself.

| Book (author)                                                | Main subject                                         |
| :----------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------- |
| Trading in the Zone (Mark Douglas)                           | probability-based thinking, psychology               |
| Trade Your Way to Financial Freedom (Van K. Tharp)           | R-multiple, expectancy, position sizing              |
| Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets (John J. Murphy) | a broad reference on technical analysis              |
| Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques (Steve Nison)       | the introductory book on candlestick patterns        |
| Come Into My Trading Room (Alexander Elder)                  | trading plan, risk, psychology                       |
| Market Wizards (Jack D. Schwager)                            | interviews with successful traders                   |
| Reminiscences of a Stock Operator (Edwin Lefèvre)            | a classic in the style of a story of a trader's life |

Before reading, check the edition and details on the publisher's page.

## Step by step: notes from one book

<Steps>

### Step 1: Write the purpose

"What do I want to know from this book?" One sentence. For example: "The method of setting risk."

### Step 2: Pull out claims as you read

2 to 5 claims in the template at the end of a chapter. Not highlights, but in your own words.

### Step 3: Mark the type

A fact, an attitude, or a testable rule.

### Step 4: A small test for the testable ones

Write the time limit, the data and the success criterion first. Then the backtest.

### Step 5: Verdict and adding to the plan

If it worked, it is a candidate for [plan v2](/en/learn/14-trading-plan-system/01-trading-plan-template). If not, write down "did not work, because...".

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **Claim:** "If you measure every trade in R, it is easier to separate luck and skill." (type: attitude + method)
- **Test:** convert 20 old trades to R and work out expectancy.
- **Verdict:** it worked; an R column was added to the journal.
- **Lesson:** a small claim, a small test, and the result went into the plan.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

- **What happened:** the trader read five books, with hundreds of highlights. They started each book's new strategy in the same week.
- **Result:** it was not clear which one worked, the plan was messy, and the results were unstable.
- **Why it failed:** many changes at once with no testing. It was impossible to separate the result of any one claim.
- **Lesson:** one claim, one test, one verdict. Then the next.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                         | Why it happens        | What to do                    |
| :---------------------------------------------- | :-------------------- | :---------------------------- |
| Treating the author's success story as proof    | The pull of a story   | Test it on your own data      |
| Only highlights                                 | It is easy            | Claims in your own words      |
| Many books at once                              | Wanting to learn fast | One book, one test            |
| Adding to the plan without testing              | Excitement            | The verdict first             |
| Thinking the numbers of an old book still apply | Markets change        | Check on your own recent data |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  At the end of each book, pick just one thing: "the one thing from this book I
  will test." Small, specific, testable.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
  If you copy a line of a book straight into your notes, keep the source and
  copyright in mind. A summary in your own words is safe and more useful.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **If the book only gives inspiration:** an attitude book cannot be tested; the goal there is a change in behavior.
- **If the examples are from an old market:** they may not apply in the present market.
- **If you have no habit of keeping notes:** even with a template it does not work; start small.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- The goal of a book is one tested change in the plan.
- Separate the claims: fact, attitude, testable rule.
- One claim, one test, one verdict.
- The author's story is not your proof.

</KeyTakeaways>
