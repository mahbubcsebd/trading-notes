# Lot Size, Margin Call and Stop-out: The Numbers That Protect Your Account

When the fuel gauge on your dashboard turns red, you head to a petrol station. A trading account has a gauge too: the **margin level**. You need to know what happens before it turns red, and how to stay well away from that point.

## What you will be able to do

- **Explain** balance, equity, margin and margin level.
- **State** what a margin call and a stop-out are.
- **Calculate** the limits for your own account.
- **Understand** the danger of oversized lots.

## Why it matters

- **A stop-out closes your positions by force.** You lose control.
- **Not knowing the margin maths causes panic.** An unexpected warning leads to bad decisions.
- **It applies [lots and leverage](/en/learn/01-foundations/03-pips-lots-leverage-margin) and [Position Sizing](/en/learn/08-risk-management/02-position-sizing) directly.**

Prerequisites: [Order Types](/en/learn/02-broker-platform-setup/04-order-types).

## Key terms

| Term         | Meaning                                                   |
| :----------- | :-------------------------------------------------------- |
| Balance      | The money in your account, including closed trades        |
| Equity       | Balance + the current profit/loss of open positions       |
| Used margin  | The deposit locked up to hold open positions              |
| Free margin  | Equity − used margin; your capacity to open new positions |
| Margin level | **(Equity ÷ used margin) × 100%**                         |

## Margin call and stop-out

- **Margin call:** When the margin level falls below a set limit (for example 100%), the broker warns you or blocks new positions.
- **Stop-out:** If it falls further (for example 50%), the broker starts **closing your positions by force**, usually starting with the one making the biggest loss.

**The limits differ by broker and account type.** The 100%/50% in this lesson are only examples. Check your own broker's terms.

## Calculation (synthetic example)

Account 1,000 USD, used margin on open positions 400 USD.

- At the start, margin level = 1,000 ÷ 400 = **250%**.
- If equity falls to 400 = **100%** (the margin call limit).
- If equity falls to 200 = **50%** (the stop-out limit).

Used margin is not always fixed (it changes as positions grow or shrink), but this is the idea.

## Step by step: calculating your own limits

<Steps>

### Step 1: Find your broker's terms

What are the margin call and stop-out levels?

### Step 2: Calculate used margin

With the margin formula. If you open several positions at once, add them up.

### Step 3: Work out the equity at margin call

Used margin × the limit. For example, 400 × 100% = 400.

### Step 4: Check the distance

How far is your equity from that level? If it is too close, reduce your lot size.

### Step 5: Stick to your risk %

At 1% per trade, you will almost never touch the margin limits. ([Risk per Trade](/en/learn/08-risk-management/01-risk-per-trade))

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **Account:** 1,000 USD, 1% risk per trade (10 USD), small lots.
- **During trading:** Six losses in a row: the account is at about 940 (slightly less). Used margin is only 40, margin level 2,000%+.
- **Result:** Nowhere near a margin call. A clear head for the next setup.
- **Lesson:** With small risk, margin is simply not a problem.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

- **Account:** 1,000 USD, used margin 400, oversized risk on every trade.
- **During trading:** Eight losing trades (the chart above): equity 1,000 → 172.
- **Result:** A margin call warning on the sixth trade, a stop-out on the eighth; positions closed by force.
- **Why it failed:** The lot size was set by "as big as possible", not by risk. Just a few losses finished the account.
- **Lesson:** The margin limit is not a safety net; it is the last line. The risk rules should have stopped things well before.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                     | Why it happens              | What to do                                                                            |
| :------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Thinking of margin as a "cost"              | Confusing terms             | It is a deposit                                                                       |
| Choosing the lot size from free margin      | "I can open this many lots" | Set the lot size by risk %                                                            |
| Not knowing the margin call/stop-out levels | Not reading the terms       | Write down the levels for each broker                                                 |
| Opening new positions as losses grow        | Trying to win it back       | [Rules for stopping](/en/learn/14-trading-plan-system/05-kokhon-trading-bondho-korbo) |
| Ignoring weekend gaps                       | Assuming the stop will work | Reduce risk before holidays                                                           |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  At the start of each day, check: what is the margin level? If it is below
  500%, limit new positions that day. (A suggested personal rule, not a
  requirement.)
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
  A stop-out can happen even with a stop loss in place, especially with big gaps
  or in fast markets. In some regions and with some brokers there is no negative
  balance protection. Know your broker's policy. This is educational material.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **Formulas differ by broker:** Some brokers calculate margin or stop-out differently.
- **With hedged positions or special accounts:** The margin calculation can differ.
- **In an account in another currency:** Exchange rates come into play.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- Margin level = (equity ÷ used margin) × 100%.
- A margin call is a warning; a stop-out closes positions by force.
- The limits differ by broker.
- If you keep to your risk %, these limits never come close.

</KeyTakeaways>
