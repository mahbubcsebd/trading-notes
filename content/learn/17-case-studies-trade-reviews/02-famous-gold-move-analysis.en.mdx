# Analysis of a Big Gold Move: How to Read It After the Event, and What You Would Do Live

"Gold rose so much last year, I did say so." After an event, everything is clear to everyone. This lesson teaches how to read a big move so that **what was known then** and **what is known now** stay apart.

**Important:** no real dates, prices or event figures are used in this lesson. The two charts are an **imaginary reconstruction**. You will check the real event yourself from reliable sources (such as publications of the World Gold Council) and put it in the same template.

## What you will be able to do

- **Read a big move** by breaking it down with **four questions**.
- **Recognize and separate hindsight bias.**
- **Say where you could have traded live**, and where not.

## Why it matters

- **The biggest mistakes happen in big moves.** Hurry, FOMO, big size. [FOMO and Revenge](/en/learn/12-trading-psychology/04-fomo-revenge-trading) does the most damage here.
- **Gold's drivers are limited to a few known names.** The ideas of the [Why Gold Moves](/en/learn/13-gold-xauusd/01-why-gold-moves) lesson are applied here to a real case.
- **Preparing for the next big event.** If the template is ready in advance, the head stays cool at the time of the event.

Prerequisites: [DXY and US Yields](/en/learn/13-gold-xauusd/03-dxy-us-yields) and [News Events](/en/learn/13-gold-xauusd/06-news-events).

## What is event analysis

**Event analysis is writing out separately the cause of a big move, its structure, the place to trade it in reality, and our bias.**

| Question      | What to write                                                                                            |
| :------------ | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. The cause? | Yield, USD, geopolitics, central bank action: which played how much?                                     |
| 2. Structure? | What was there before the move (a range)? What kind of move (simple, a jump)? What after (a higher low)? |
| 3. Live?      | At what price could one have entered then, how big a stop, and why?                                      |
| 4. Bias?      | "What I know now, I did not know then": where is the gap?                                                |

## Step by step: the analysis

<Steps>

### Step 1: The state before the move

How small was the range? What were the [session and volatility](/en/learn/13-gold-xauusd/05-sessions-volatility) like? A big move comes after stillness, but when, cannot be known in advance.

### Step 2: The moment of the trigger

Which news or event? The rule of the News lesson: do not enter at the moment of the spike.

### Step 3: Look across markets

What are the yield and USD doing at the same time? This lets you test the cause behind the move.

### Step 4: Find the place to trade live

The pullback and higher low after the spike. A small stop, a clear structure.

### Step 5: Write your own bias

"Would I have entered on the spike?" Write the honest answer. That is the lesson for the next event.

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **Context:** a long range, then a jump on news; the yield was falling at the same time.
- **Live:** did not enter on the spike. Waited, higher low 2341. Buy 2341, stop 2327 (14 points), target 2369 (28 points).
- **Result:** the target was hit, +2R.
- **Lesson:** traded a small part of a big move, where the stop was small and the cause clear. There is no need to catch the whole move.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

## Example 2: when it fails (synthetic)

- **What happened:** FOMO on seeing the spike. Buy at 2347.36, stop 2335.36, target 2371.36.
- **Result:** the stop was hit on the pullback, -1R. Then price rose further.
- **Why it failed:** the direction was right, but the entry price was at the worst place. A normal pullback alone touched the stop.
- **Lesson:** being right on direction is not enough; the entry price and the place of the stop must be right too.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                          | Why it happens              | What to do                                                                  |
| :----------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Knowing" the cause after the event              | Hindsight bias              | Write separately "what was known then"                                      |
| Chasing right after the spike                    | FOMO                        | Wait for a higher low or retest                                             |
| Explaining everything with one cause             | The pull of a simple story  | Keep several possible causes                                                |
| Not checking the facts of a real event           | Trusting online posts       | Check against reliable sources                                              |
| Raising size, thinking "I'll catch it this time" | Regret over an earlier miss | Keep the [risk rules](/en/learn/08-risk-management/01-risk-per-trade) fixed |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  After every big event, write a one-page analysis: four questions, short
  answers. After five events you will see the pattern of your own bias.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
  The prices, dates and causes of real events are not in this lesson, on
  purpose. Do not build an analysis on unchecked information. This is
  educational writing, not investment advice.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **Live, at the time of the event:** the analysis is after the event; at the moment of the spike there is no time to think this much. That is why the rules come first.
- **If the cause is unclear:** not every move has a clear cause.
- **With few events:** no general conclusion from two or three cases.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- Four questions: cause, structure, the place live, bias.
- Not on the spike, but on the higher low or retest.
- Write hindsight bias separately.
- Check the real facts yourself; the charts here are imaginary.

</KeyTakeaways>
