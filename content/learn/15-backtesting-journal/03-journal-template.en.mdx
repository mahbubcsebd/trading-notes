# Journal Template: Which Columns, Why, and How to Fill Them

In [Journaling Habit](/en/learn/12-trading-psychology/05-journaling-habit) you learned why the habit of keeping a journal is needed. Here is its structure: which columns there will be, when they are filled, and which metric comes from which column.

## What you will be able to do

- **Divide the journal into three parts:** **before, during and after**.
- **Say which question** each column **answers**.
- **Build a spreadsheet template** yourself.

## Why it matters

- **You need data that can be matched.** To work out [metrics](/en/learn/15-backtesting-journal/02-performance-metrics-forward-testing), every trade has to be written in the same format.
- **Memory mixes up luck and skill.** A log with screenshots and reasons is an honest witness.
- **A review is possible only when there is data.** The next lesson runs on the journal.

Prerequisites: [Daily and Weekly Routine](/en/learn/14-trading-plan-system/04-daily-weekly-routine).

## What is a journal

**A trade journal is a written record of every trade in the same format, from which the result and the pattern of mistakes can be found later.**

Think of a doctor's patient file. The same boxes are filled each time, so after six months a trend can be seen.

## A three-part template

| Part   | When                    | Columns                                                                                                          | What it answers                  |
| :----- | :---------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| Before | Right before entry      | date-time, instrument, setup name, direction, entry, stop, target, risk %, checklist passed (yes/no), screenshot | What was the plan?               |
| During | While the trade is open | any change (was the stop/target moved?), mental state (1–5)                                                      | Were the rules broken?           |
| After  | When closed             | exit price, result (R), tag, the reason in one line, the final screenshot                                        | What happened, what did I learn? |

A **tag** is a short label: `Plan`, `FOMO`, `Moved stop`, `Revenge`, `No-checklist`. One main tag for a trade.

## Step by step: building a spreadsheet

<Steps>

### Step 1: Set the columns

The columns of the table above, in one row. Each trade is one row.

### Step 2: Put in the formula for R

Result (R) = (exit − entry) ÷ (entry − stop), for a buy. For a sell the sign flips. Automate it with a formula.

### Step 3: Set the list of tags

4–6 tags in a drop-down. Keep the list fixed instead of making a new tag each time.

### Step 4: A summary page

On a separate page: total trades, win rate, expectancy, total R by tag. Link them with formulas.

### Step 5: The rule for screenshots

Before entry and after exit. Name the file date-instrument.

### Step 6: Fill it in every day

15 minutes after the window. If you leave it for the next day, it gets dropped.

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

- **What there was:** a tag, a checklist and a screenshot on every trade.
- **Result:** at the end of the month, counting by tag: the net positive in `Plan` trades, and the net negative in `FOMO` and `Moved stop` trades.
- **Decision:** next month's goal, bring FOMO trades down to zero.
- **Lesson:** with tags the problem became specific; not "I'll do better" but "I'll drop FOMO".

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade.)

- **What happened:** another trader wrote only the date and the result. No tag, checklist or reason.
- **Result:** at the end of the month the total was negative, but the cause could not be found. They changed the strategy, though the real problem was breaking the rules.
- **Why it failed:** the data had no reason column, so the fix was in the wrong place.
- **Lesson:** writing the result is half the job. Without reasons and tags, a journal is only an accounts book.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                    | Why it happens              | What to do                           |
| :------------------------- | :-------------------------- | :----------------------------------- |
| Writing only the wins      | Avoiding the pain of losses | Every trade, even the skipped ones   |
| Too many columns           | The urge to be perfect      | Stop at 12–15 columns                |
| A new tag each time        | Lack of habit               | A fixed drop-down list               |
| Writing from memory later  | Laziness                    | Fill in the "before" part beforehand |
| Not looking at the summary | Writing feels like the work | Open it in the weekly review         |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  Write the "skipped trade" in a separate row, with the reason. A trade left out
  by the rules is also proof of your discipline.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
  The most valuable column of the journal is often the "reason in one line". You
  will forget the result, and you will not remember the reason, so write it
  down.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **If the journal is a formality:** if you fill the boxes without thinking, the data misleads.
- **If there are so many columns that the habit does not last:** start small and add if needed.
- **If you change the tag before seeing the result:** give the tag honestly after the trade, not on the basis of the result.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- The template has three parts: before, during, after.
- The result in R, the tag from a fixed list.
- The reason column is the most valuable.
- Write the skipped trades too.

</KeyTakeaways>
