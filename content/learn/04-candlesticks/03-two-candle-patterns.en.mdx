# Two Candle Patterns: The Second Candle Answers the First

In the previous lesson, one candle gave you a suspicion. Now that suspicion needs a witness. The second candle is that witness: did it agree with what the first said, or turn it around? Reading two candles together makes the picture much clearer, but there are plenty of traps too.

## What you will be able to do

- **Recognise** the engulfing, harami and tweezer, and say how they differ.
- **Read** two candles by **combining their bodies, wicks and closes**.
- **Place** a stop-loss using the pattern's high or low.
- **Avoid** the situations where these patterns become traps.

## Why it matters

- **One candle is often unclear.** The second candle shows whether the market is really changing direction or has just paused.
- **The pattern's shape sets the stop.** The engulfing's low or the harami's low tells you where price will go if you are proven wrong.
- **The road to three-candle and larger patterns starts here.** The morning star and three white soldiers are all extensions of this logic.

Prerequisites: [Single Candle Patterns](/en/learn/04-candlesticks/02-single-candle-patterns) and [Trend and Range](/en/learn/03-market-structure/02-trend-range-hhhl).

## What a two-candle pattern is

**A two-candle pattern is the relationship between two candles in a row, where the second candle gives new information by comparing with the first one's body and close.**

Think of it as a question and an answer. The first candle asked: "Who is winning?" The second candle answered. The answer can be one of three kinds: it turned everything around (engulfing), it stopped (harami), or it got stuck at the same place (tweezer).

Remember the formula: **Pattern = shape + location + confirmation.** No exceptions here either.

## Three patterns, three answers

| Pattern       | What the second candle does                                                        | What it means                       |
| :------------ | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------- |
| **Engulfing** | Covers the first candle's whole body, in the opposite colour                       | Control may be changing hands       |
| **Harami**    | Stays small inside the body of the first, big candle                               | Momentum has slowed, a pause        |
| **Tweezer**   | Touches about the same high (top) or same low (bottom) as the first and turns back | Resistance or support at that price |

Each one has its own rules.

### Engulfing: the body gets covered

In a bullish engulfing, the first candle is bearish and the second is bullish. The second candle's body fully covers the first one's body: its open is at or below the first one's close, and its close is at or above the first one's open. A bearish engulfing is the exact opposite.

**Many people think the wicks must be covered too, but under the common rule only the body is checked.** Some books and platforms strictly use the full range, so keep one rule for yourself.

### Harami: a small candle inside

"Harami" means pregnant woman in Japanese. Remember it as "a small candle inside a big candle". The first candle has a big body; the second candle's body is inside it. It is the exact opposite of the engulfing: first strength, then indecision.

A harami is **not a reversal on its own**. It says "momentum has slowed". The next candle proves it.

### Tweezer: pushed back at the same price

In a tweezer top, two candles touch about the same high and price comes back. In a tweezer bottom, the same low. When it comes at the end of an existing trend, it suggests the market has been pushed back at that price again.

How close "about the same" has to be is not strict. A very small gap compared with the timeframe's normal range is acceptable. To see the detailed forms of all three patterns, look in the [Pattern Library](/en/patterns).

<Callout type="note">
  All three mean something only after a trend. In the middle of a range they
  form again and again and say nothing.
</Callout>

## Step by step: how to read a two-candle pattern

<Steps>

### Step 1: Look at the trend first

Which way did the last 8–10 candles go? If you want a bullish pattern, you need a downtrend first.

### Step 2: Find a location

Is there [support or resistance](/en/learn/05-support-resistance/01-levels-vs-zones) nearby? Without a location, a pattern is just a shape.

### Step 3: Measure the second candle's close

In an engulfing, is the body really covered? In a harami, is the small candle inside? In a tweezer, are the highs or lows about equal?

### Step 4: Wait for the candle to close

A running second candle changes. Do not even name the pattern until it closes.

### Step 5: Ask for confirmation

For a bullish pattern, the next candle closes above the pattern's high. For a bearish one, below the low.

### Step 6: Set the stop first

The stop goes outside the whole pattern: for a bullish one, a little below the lowest low of the two candles; for a bearish one, a little above the highest high. Set your [position size](/en/learn/08-risk-management/02-position-sizing) from the stop distance. See also [Stop Loss Placement](/en/learn/10-trade-management-execution/01-stop-loss-placement).

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

Look at the chart above.

- **What could be seen:** a steady downtrend; price had bounced once at the 1.0745 support. Candle 7 was bearish, then candle 8 opened below candle 7's close and closed above candle 7's open.
- **Decision:** shape and location match, but the entry is not yet.
- **Confirmation:** candle 9 closed above the engulfing's high (1.0790). Buy at about 1.0792. The stop a little below the engulfing's low (1.0730), at 1.0722.
- **Result:** price rose step by step.
- **Lesson:** the stop was about 70 pips away. A stop that far means a small position size. The bigger the engulfing candle, the higher the risk.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade, and no result is guaranteed.)

## Example 2: when it becomes a trap (synthetic)

<Chart
  id="two-candle-harami-fail-01"
  height={420}
  caption="EURUSD H4 (educational, synthetic example) — a bullish harami in the middle of a downtrend with no support nearby; price breaks its low and falls further"
/>

- **What could be seen:** a run of bearish candles, with candle 5 a big one. Candle 6, a small candle, sits inside its body: a textbook bullish harami.
- **Wrong decision:** "A harami in a downtrend, now the reversal." Confidence grew when candle 7 closed above the harami. Buy at 1.0810, stop at 1.0770.
- **What happened:** candle 8 fell hard and hit the stop. Then came three more bearish candles.
- **Why it failed:** a harami means a pause, not a turn. There was no support nearby. The downtrend was still strong. One small bullish candle and one close after it were not enough proof.

**Lesson:** when you see a harami, ask "why did it stop?", not "why did it turn?". Because the stop had been set in advance, the loss was held to 40 pips.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                          | Why it happens                                | What to do                                        |
| :----------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| Treating a harami as a reversal                  | The name is familiar, the shape is attractive | Think of it as a pause, wait for a break          |
| Judging an engulfing by the wicks                | Some books use a different rule               | Write down your own rule, stick to the body       |
| Looking for patterns in the middle of a range    | Shapes match everywhere                       | Check the trend and level first                   |
| Entering after an engulfing without confirmation | Fear of missing an open chance                | Wait for the next candle's close                  |
| Entering straight off an extra-large engulfing   | Confidence from the shape                     | The stop is far; make the size smaller or skip it |
| Measuring a tweezer's "about equal" your own way | There is no strict definition                 | Measure it against the timeframe's range          |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  The smaller the candle before the engulfing and the bigger the second candle,
  the more dramatic it is. But extra-dramatic candles are often the result of
  news. Be careful there.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
  Watch for the break after a harami. In a bullish harami, it matters only if
  price breaks the small candle's high; if it does not, nothing has changed.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
  Fixed rules like "engulfing = reversal" do not always hold in any market. If
  someone tells you a specific success percentage, do not believe it without
  checking.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **When there is no trend:** inside a range these patterns come again and again, and nothing changes.
- **In empty space with no level:** as in Example 2.
- **On small timeframes or in low liquidity:** on M1–M5 the shapes are random.
- **During big news:** extra-large candles are artificial, not the market's normal view.
- **When the earlier candle is very small:** "engulfing" a small candle is easy, but meaningless.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- The second candle answers the first: the engulfing turns it around, the harami stops, the tweezer gets stuck at the same price.
- In an engulfing the body is covered, not the wick. A harami is a pause, not a reversal.
- All three mean something after a trend and near a level.
- The stop goes outside the whole pattern; set the size from the stop distance.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Quiz

1. In a bullish engulfing, which part of the first candle does the second candle cover?
2. What is the main difference between a harami and an engulfing?
3. Why is buying immediately after seeing a harami risky?
4. Where does the stop go in a bullish engulfing?

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1.** The body. Under the common rule, the second candle's body fully covers the first one's body; the wicks are not counted.

**2.** In an engulfing, the second candle is big and covers the first; in a harami, the second candle is small and inside the first. One is a change of strength, the other a pause.

**3.** A harami is only a hint that momentum has slowed, not proof of a turn. The trend can still continue.

**4.** A little below the lowest low of the two candles.

</details>
