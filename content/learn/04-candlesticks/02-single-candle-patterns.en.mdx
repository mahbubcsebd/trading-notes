# Single Candle Patterns: Read Shape Plus Location, Not Shape Alone

You saw a nice hammer on the chart and bought. Price fell further and hit your stop. The next day you saw another candle of the same shape and let it go, and that one was the real reversal. The two candles looked exactly alike. The difference was just one thing: where they formed.

## What you will be able to do

- **Recognise** six single-candle patterns (doji, hammer, hanging man, inverted hammer, shooting star, marubozu).
- **Explain** why a candle of the same shape **gets a different name depending on the trend**.
- **Use** a simple **reading method** that covers the pattern, confirmation and stop-loss.
- **Avoid** the places where these patterns become traps.

## Why it matters

- **These catch the eye most.** Because they are the easiest to spot on a chart, new traders chase them first.
- **Chasing names loses money.** Rules like "see a hammer, buy" do not work if you ignore location.
- **They let you place a stop-loss.** The pattern's wick tells you where price will go if your idea is proven wrong.

Prerequisite: [Anatomy of a Candle](/en/learn/04-candlesticks/01-anatomy-of-a-candle) (body, wick, open, close). To recognise trends, see [Trend and Range](/en/learn/03-market-structure/02-trend-range-hhhl).

## What a single-candle pattern is

**A single-candle pattern is the shape of just one candle, from which you can tell which side won or who was pushed back during that time.**

Think of it like a doctor's first glance. Seeing the colour of a patient's face, the doctor has a suspicion, but does not prescribe medicine. First they learn the history and order tests. A candle's shape is also just a suspicion. Trend and location are the patient's history.

Remember the formula:

**Pattern = shape + location (trend and level) + confirmation.**

If one of the three is missing, it is not a pattern yet, just a shape.

## Four basic shapes

First just learn the shapes. Here "long" means the common rule of thumb: the wick is at least twice the body. This is not a strict rule; it varies slightly between books and platforms.

| Shape                     | What it looks like                                          | What it says                             |
| :------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- |
| **Doji**                  | open ≈ close, almost no body                                | Indecision; neither side won             |
| **Marubozu**              | no wicks or very small ones, a big body                     | One side in control from start to finish |
| **Hammer shape**          | small body at the top, long lower wick, short upper wick    | It went down and buyers brought it back  |
| **Inverted-hammer shape** | small body at the bottom, long upper wick, short lower wick | It went up and sellers brought it back   |

Four shapes, yet six patterns. Where are the other two? This is the key trick.

## Same shape, two names: the trend decides

The name of the hammer shape and of the inverted-hammer shape each depends on the **trend before it**.

| Shape                                   | After a downtrend                               | After an uptrend                              |
| :-------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- |
| Long lower wick (hammer shape)          | **Hammer** (possible bullish reversal)          | **Hanging man** (possible bearish reversal)   |
| Long upper wick (inverted-hammer shape) | **Inverted hammer** (possible bullish reversal) | **Shooting star** (possible bearish reversal) |

So you do not need to memorise four names for shapes. Learn two shapes, then ask: "Which way was it going before?"

<Callout type="note">
  The names of the doji and marubozu do not depend on the trend. But their
  meaning changes with location. A doji at support says one thing; in empty
  space it says another.
</Callout>

## Step by step: how to read a single-candle pattern

<Steps>

### Step 1: Look at the trend first

Which way did the last 8–10 candles go? If there is no trend, there is no such thing as a reversal pattern.

### Step 2: Find a location

Is there [support or resistance](/en/learn/05-support-resistance/01-levels-vs-zones) nearby? If the pattern is not on or near that location, raise your suspicion.

### Step 3: Measure the shape

Is the wick at least twice the body? Is the candle not small compared with those around it? A wick measured among tiny candles means nothing.

### Step 4: Wait for the candle to close

A running candle changes every second. Do not name it until it finishes.

### Step 5: Ask for confirmation

After a bullish pattern, if the next candle does not close above the pattern's high, treat the signal as weak. For a bearish pattern, below the low.

### Step 6: Set the stop first

The stop goes beyond the pattern's wick (for a hammer, a little below the low; for a shooting star, a little above the high). Set your [position size](/en/learn/08-risk-management/02-position-sizing) based on how many pips away the stop is, not the other way round. See also [Stop Loss Placement](/en/learn/10-trade-management-execution/01-stop-loss-placement).

</Steps>

## Example 1: when it works (synthetic)

Look at the chart above.

- **What could be seen:** a long downtrend; price had bounced from the 1.0750 support before. Candle 10 dropped below support and closed near its high.
- **Decision:** shape and location both match. But the entry is not yet. Wait for the next candle.
- **Confirmation:** candle 11 closed above the hammer's high (1.0752). Buy here, with the stop a little below the wick's low (1.0702), at 1.0695.
- **Result:** price rose step by step.
- **Lesson:** the stop distance was about 55 pips. So a small size had to be taken. The longer the wick, the farther the stop and the smaller the size.

(Synthetic educational example. This is not a real trade, and no result is guaranteed.)

## Example 2: when it becomes a trap (synthetic)

/>

- **What could be seen:** a run of bullish candles, then a long upper wick on candle 6. A textbook shooting star.
- **Wrong decision:** "A shooting star at the end of an uptrend, let me sell." Candle 7 broke the star's low (1.0938), so the sell was triggered.
- **What happened:** the next candle closed above the star's high (1.0990). The stop (1.0995) was hit.
- **Why it failed:** the shape was right, but the location was not. There was no resistance nearby, and the trend was steady. The wick was just a brief pushback.

**Lesson:** without a location, a shooting star is just a shape. This loss was small because the stop had been set in advance.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                       | Why it happens                       | What to do                                     |
| :-------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------- |
| Entering on the shape alone                   | The name is familiar and easy to see | Check the trend and level first                |
| Entering without confirmation                 | Rushing, fear of missing out         | Wait for the next candle's close               |
| Mixing up the hammer and hanging man          | The shape is the same                | Check the trend first, then name it            |
| Calling a reversal from any doji              | A doji means indecision, not a turn  | Watch which way the candle after the doji goes |
| Treating small-timeframe patterns as big ones | All candles seem equal               | Prefer H1, H4, D1; avoid M1–M5                 |
| Placing the stop inside the wick              | A small stop feels good              | Stop beyond the wick, make the size smaller    |

## Pro tips

<Callout type="tip">
  When two or three patterns come one after another at the same location, they
  carry more weight. When a pattern comes alone, stay suspicious.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
  Do not see the marubozu only as a sign of strength. At the end of a big trend,
  an extra-large marubozu is often exhaustion. Check the location.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
  Fixed rules like "hammer = buy" and "shooting star = sell" do not work in the
  market. Be careful if anyone guarantees you a success rate for a pattern.
</Callout>

## When this does not work

- **When there is no trend (in a range or choppy market):** for a reversal, something has to be moving first.
- **In empty space with no level:** as in Example 2.
- **On small timeframes or in low liquidity:** the shapes are almost random.
- **During big news:** wicks stretch artificially, which is not the market's normal view.
- **On very small candles:** if the candle is smaller than those around it, a "long" wick is really nothing.

## Summary

<KeyTakeaways>

- Pattern = shape + location + confirmation. If one is missing, it is just a shape.
- Two shapes (long lower wick, long upper wick) get four names depending on the trend: hammer, hanging man, inverted hammer, shooting star.
- A doji is indecision, a marubozu is control. Neither is a reversal on its own.
- The stop goes beyond the wick; set the size from the stop distance.

</KeyTakeaways>

## Quiz

1. What is a candle of the same shape called after an uptrend, and after a downtrend?
2. When you see a shooting star, which two things should you check first?
3. What does a doji indicate?
4. Where does a hammer's stop go?

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1.** For a long lower wick: after an uptrend, a hanging man; after a downtrend, a hammer.

**2.** Whether the trend before it was up, and whether there is resistance nearby.

**3.** Indecision. The open and close are almost equal; neither side won. On its own it is not a reversal.

**4.** A little below the hammer's low. Not inside the wick.

</details>
